Beach Safety Checklist — Hawaiʻi

A quick “before you swim” run-through for guests. The same core rules hold on every beach; each island then has a few things that catch visitors out.

Print any single page as a room card or front-desk leave-behind — each island fits one page. Screen view scrolls through all three.

OceanSafe · Beach Safety
Oʻahu
Before you get in the water
Every beach, every time
  • Find a lifeguard tower and swim in front of a staffed one.
  • Never swim alone. Keep a buddy in sight.
  • Watch keiki within arm’s reach. Calm, shallow water still drowns.
  • Ask the lifeguard what the water is doing today, or check conditions before you go.
  • Never turn your back to the ocean. The big wave in a set comes without warning.
  • Caught in a current? Don’t fight it. Stay calm, float, swim across it toward the beach, and wave for help.
Oʻahu — know before you go
  • Winter (about October–April), the North Shore and west side get huge, dangerous surf. If the surf is up, stay out and watch from the sand.
  • Sandy Beach and Makapuʻu shorebreak land right on the sand and hurt strong swimmers. Sit those out unless you really know shorebreak.
  • Many drownings near Waikīkī happen offshore and are medical, not the waves. Don’t swim alone and stay within your depth.
  • Deep channels sit right next to shallow reef (like Ala Moana). Keep weak swimmers on the reef flat.
  • Resort lagoons (Ko Olina) usually have no lifeguard. Calm water still drowns young kids and older swimmers.
  • Give sea life room — harassing them is illegal. Keep 10 ft from honu (turtles), 50 ft from monk seals.
Emergency: call 911, ask for Ocean Safety When in doubt, don’t go out. oceansafety.app

Maui — know before you go
  • Snorkeling causes more visitor ocean deaths than anything else. Snorkel close to shore, never alone, and get out if you feel cold, tired, or short of breath.
  • Calm-looking bays can pull hard. Off Black Rock (Kāʻanapali), Kapalua, and Nāpili, currents pick up when swell wraps in.
  • Winter north swells and summer south swells turn easy family beaches into dumping shorebreak. Check conditions first.
  • Where there’s a buoyed swim zone (Kanahā), stay inside it. The water outside runs offshore.
  • Many West and South Maui beaches have no lifeguard. Pick a guarded beach when you can, especially with kids.
  • Reef and rock are sharp and shallow. Wear reef shoes and don’t stand on the coral.

Hawaiʻi Island
Before you get in the water
Every beach, every time

Hawaiʻi Island — know before you go
  • More lava rock than sand. Entries are sharp and uneven — wear reef shoes and pick calm days.
  • Many beaches are remote and unguarded (Green Sand, Makalawena, Two Step). No lifeguard and weak or no cell signal — bring water and tell someone your plan.
  • Hilo/Keaukaha tidepools (Richardson, Onekahakaha, Carlsmith) look calm but have channels and currents. Keep keiki close and in the protected pools.
  • Lava-entry snorkel spots like Two Step are for calm days only. Get in and out at the steps, not over the reef.
  • Surf spots like Honoliʻi are for experienced surfers, not for swimming.
  • On any rocky shore, stay well back from wet rock. A single sweeper wave can pull you in.
