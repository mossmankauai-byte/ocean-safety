Maui Surf Season: When Each Shore Is Calm (and When It Is Not)

Maui does not have one ocean season. It has several at once, and which shore is calm flips depending on the month. North and west facing beaches get pounded by winter swells that arrive from storms in the North Pacific. South shore beaches get their surf in summer from swells born far to the south. Knowing which coast is working on a given week is the difference between an easy snorkel and a scary one.

Quick facts

  • North and west shores are biggest November through March from North Pacific swells.
  • South shores (Kihei, Wailea, Mākena) get their surf April through September from south swells.
  • Honolua Bay is a winter surf break and not safe for snorkeling when north swell is running.
  • Big Beach at Mākena has a dangerous shorebreak that injures people even on small summer days.
  • The north shore (Hoʻokipa, Paia) is windy and current-prone across much of the year.
  • Molokini and the south/west protected beaches are the more reliable winter snorkel zones.

Winter: north and west shores get big

From roughly November through March, and sometimes into April, North Pacific swells send surf to the north and west shores. Honolua Bay turns into a world-class surf break and is no place to snorkel then. Kapalua, Kaʻanapali, and the Lahaina-side beaches can go from flat to overhead within a day when a swell fills in. Surf forecasts are your friend in winter, because a beach that was glassy yesterday can have a dangerous shorebreak and strong currents today. Check Honolua Bay conditions and D.T. Fleming before you decide.

Summer: the south shore gets its turn

From about April through September, Southern Hemisphere swells reach the south-facing shores. That means Kihei, Wailea, and Mākena see more surf and shorebreak in summer, while the north and west shores usually calm down. South swells are generally smaller than winter north swells, but Big Beach (Oneloa) at Mākena is known for a powerful shorebreak that snaps ankles and dislocates shoulders even on modest days. See Big Beach conditions before you get in.

The north shore is windy most of the year

Hoʻokipa and the Paia stretch face both swell and the prevailing trade winds, so they tend to be choppy and current-prone across many months, not just one season. Hoʻokipa is a surf and windsurf spot with rocks and rip currents, better for watching than swimming for most visitors. Baby Beach in Paia is the calmer exception, protected by a reef, but it is a keiki spot, not an open-water swim.

Where it stays relatively calm

Molokini crater and the Wailea and Kihei beaches are the more reliably protected snorkel zones in winter, which is why most snorkel boats run the south and west side then. Napili Bay and Kapalua Bay are semi-protected pockets on the west side that can still be worth it between swells. Relatively calm is not the same as safe. Any of these can turn with a new swell or an offshore wind, so treat each day on its own.

How to read a Maui day

Look at the surf forecast for the shore you are visiting, not the island as a whole. Watch the water for a few minutes before entering and count the waves in a set. If a beach has lifeguards, ask them, since they know that specific break. When in doubt, pick a shore that is out of season for surf: south shore in winter, west or north-protected coves in summer. For the bigger picture on timing a trip, see best time to visit Maui and the month-by-month weather guide.

Before you go

  • A calm beach can turn dangerous within a day when a new swell fills in. Check the forecast for that specific shore before every swim.
  • Big Beach shorebreak and Hoʻokipa currents injure and drown strong swimmers. Watch the water, and stay out if waves are breaking hard on sand or rock.
  • Surf forecasts describe open-ocean swell, not what a rip current is doing at your feet. Defer to lifeguards and live conditions over any seasonal rule of thumb.
