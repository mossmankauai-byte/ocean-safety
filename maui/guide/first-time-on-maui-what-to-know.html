First Time on Maui: What to Know Before You Land

Maui rewards a little planning more than most places. A few of the best experiences now need reservations booked weeks out, the ocean is less forgiving than it looks, and one part of the island is still rebuilding after the 2023 fire. Here is what a first-timer actually needs to know before the plane doors open.

Quick facts

  • You will need a rental car; book it early, along with any Haleakalā sunrise and Waiʻānapanapa reservations.
  • Haleakalā entrance is $30 per vehicle for 3 days, plus a $1 sunrise reservation; Waiʻānapanapa is $5/person and $10/vehicle.
  • Much of Lahaina town remains closed and rebuilding after the 2023 fire; do not visit burn areas.
  • Maui County allows only mineral sunscreen; chemical sunscreens with oxybenzone or octinoxate are banned statewide.
  • Cell service is unreliable on the Road to Hāna and near the Haleakalā summit; download offline maps.
  • The Haleakalā summit can be near freezing at sunrise while the coast stays warm.

You will need a rental car, and reservations

Maui has almost no useful public transit for visitors, so plan on a rental car and book it early. Beyond the car, two of the signature outings need timed reservations: Haleakalā sunrise (a $1-per-vehicle reservation on top of the $30 park entrance fee) and Waiʻānapanapa State Park on the Road to Hāna ($5 per person plus $10 per vehicle, prepaid). Both open a set number of days ahead and sell out. See the sunrise reservation guide for the exact timing.

Respect Lahaina, it is still rebuilding

The August 2023 wildfire destroyed much of Lahaina town, and in 2026 the historic Front Street area remains largely closed to through traffic while infrastructure and storefronts are rebuilt. This is a community in recovery, not a sightseeing stop. Do not drive in to look at burn areas or photograph damaged sites. The Kāʻanapali and Kapalua resort areas nearby are fully open and welcoming visitors, and a growing number of Lahaina-area restaurants have reopened.

The ocean deserves real respect

Maui's water can look calm and turn dangerous fast, with strong currents, shore break, and big surf that shifts by season and shore. No beach is ever simply safe. Check lifeguards and live conditions every time, and read the most dangerous beaches rundown before you pick a spot. Our snorkel safety guide covers where people most often get into trouble.

Distances are longer than they look

The island is bigger than the map suggests, and the roads wind. Getting from Kīhei to Kāʻanapali can take an hour, and the Road to Hāna is a full-day commitment, not a morning drive. Cell service drops out on the east side and up the volcano, so download offline maps. The driving times guide lays out realistic estimates.

Sunscreen rules are stricter here

Hawaiʻi bans the sale of sunscreens with oxybenzone and octinoxate statewide, and Maui County goes further, allowing only mineral (non-nano zinc oxide or titanium dioxide) sunscreens. Bring reef-safe mineral sunscreen from home or buy it locally. The reef-safe sunscreen guide explains exactly what is allowed.

Pack for more than the beach

The Haleakalā summit can sit near freezing at sunrise while the coast is warm, and the Hāna side is often wet. Bring a warm layer and a rain shell, plus water shoes for rocky entries. The packing guide covers the cold parts people forget.

Before you go

  • No Maui beach is safe by default. Currents, shore break, and surf change with the season and the day, so check lifeguards and live conditions before every swim or snorkel.
  • Lahaina is a fire-recovery community, not an attraction. Stay out of closed and burn areas and give residents privacy.
  • The Haleakalā summit sits at 10,000 feet. Altitude, cold, and dark predawn driving catch first-timers off guard, so prepare for all three.
