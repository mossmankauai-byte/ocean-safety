Reef-Safe Sunscreen on Maui: What Act 104 Means for Your Bag

You may have heard Maui has a sunscreen law and packed accordingly. The reality is two overlapping rules, and the local one is stricter than the statewide Act 104 most people know about. Getting it right is simple once you know what to look for on the label.

Quick facts

  • Hawaiʻi's Act 104 (effective January 2021) bans the sale of sunscreens with oxybenzone and octinoxate statewide.
  • Maui County Ordinance 5306 (effective October 2022) goes further, allowing only mineral (non-nano zinc oxide or titanium dioxide) sunscreens.
  • On Maui, mineral-only is the standard for your bag; chemical UV filters do not comply.
  • Terms like "reef-safe" are unregulated, so read the active-ingredient panel, not the front label.
  • Compliant mineral sunscreen is sold in local shops, so you can buy it on arrival.

Two laws, and the stricter one wins

Hawaiʻi's Act 104, passed in 2018 and in effect since January 1, 2021, bans the sale and distribution of sunscreens containing oxybenzone and octinoxate statewide without a prescription. That is the law people usually mean by "reef-safe." But Maui County went further with Ordinance 5306, in effect since October 1, 2022, which allows only mineral sunscreens made with non-nano zinc oxide or titanium dioxide. On Maui, the county rule is what governs your bag.

What that means in practice

On Maui, the safe move is a mineral sunscreen whose only active ingredients are non-nano zinc oxide or titanium dioxide. Chemical sunscreens, including those with oxybenzone, octinoxate, avobenzone, octocrylene, and similar filters, are the ones to leave home. If a bottle lists a chemical UV filter as an active ingredient, it does not meet the Maui standard.

The ban covers sale, distribution, and use of non-mineral sunscreen without a prescription, so this is not only about what stores stock, it is about what you rub on before you get in the water near a reef.

How to read the label

Flip the bottle to the active ingredients panel. You want zinc oxide, titanium dioxide, or both, and ideally the label will say non-nano. Marketing terms like "reef-friendly" or "reef-safe" are not regulated and do not guarantee compliance, so ignore the front of the bottle and read the back. When in doubt, mineral-only is the rule to remember.

Where to buy it

Local shops, pharmacies, and grocery stores on Maui stock compliant mineral sunscreen, so you do not have to bring it if you would rather buy on arrival. If you already have a favorite mineral brand at home, pack it. Bringing a non-compliant chemical sunscreen is not worth the trouble, and you will just want the mineral kind here anyway.

Cut how much you need

The most reef-friendly approach is to wear less sunscreen and cover up more. A rash guard or sun shirt, a hat, and shade do most of the work, and you reserve mineral sunscreen for the parts you cannot cover. That is easier on the reef and on you. Our packing guide covers sun shirts and water gear.

Why it matters here

Oxybenzone and octinoxate have been linked to coral damage, including bleaching and harm to developing coral, and elevated levels have been measured at popular Maui reefs like Honolua Bay. If you are snorkeling to see coral and sea turtles, the sunscreen on your skin is part of what keeps those reefs alive. Check live conditions before you get in, and read the snorkel safety guide first.

Before you go

  • A prescription exemption exists for medically necessary non-mineral sunscreen, but for general beach use, mineral-only is the rule on Maui.
  • Sunscreen choice does not change ocean risk. Reef and rocky entries, currents, and surf are still hazards, so check lifeguards and live conditions before you get in.
  • Non-nano mineral sunscreen is preferred; nano particles are excluded under the Maui County rule, so look for non-nano on the label.
