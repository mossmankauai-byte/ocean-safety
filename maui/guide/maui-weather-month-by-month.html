Maui Weather Month by Month: What to Actually Expect

Maui's weather is mild all year, so the differences between months are smaller than in most places. What changes is the odds of rain, the size of the surf, and how many other people are on the beach. Here is a realistic month-by-month read, plus why the number on your phone often lies about your actual beach.

Quick facts

  • Coastal highs stay mild year-round: roughly low 80s °F in winter, mid-to-upper 80s in summer.
  • Leeward Maui (Lahaina, Kīhei, Wailea) averages only about 10–15 inches of rain a year; Hāna gets around 400.
  • December is the wettest month on average; May–October is the dry season, with June among the driest.
  • Ocean temperatures range from the mid-70s °F in winter to near 80 in late summer.
  • North-shore surf peaks in winter; south-shore surf is larger in summer.

First, forget one island-wide forecast

Maui has many microclimates packed into one island. West and South Maui coasts (Lahaina, Kīhei, Wailea) are dry, averaging only about 10–15 inches of rain a year. Upcountry and the east side are far wetter, and Hāna gets on the order of 400 inches annually. A rainy forecast for Maui usually means the mountains and east side, not your south-shore beach. January–March: cooler, wetter, whales

These are the coolest, wettest months, though cool here still means coastal highs around the low 80s Fahrenheit. December through February see the most rain, largely on the windward and up-mountain sides, and the occasional winter Kona storm can flip the usual pattern and wet the south and west. North-shore surf is at its biggest. Whales peak now. Water temperatures dip to around the mid-70s. See the whale guide.

April–June: drying out and warming up

Spring shifts the island toward its dry season. Rain tapers through April and May, and June is one of Maui's driest, sunniest months with steady trade winds. Air and water warm gradually. This stretch is comfortable and increasingly reliable, and it lands before the summer crowd peak. It is a strong window for calm-water beach days on the leeward coasts.

July–September: hot, dry, and calm

Summer is the driest and warmest period, with coastal highs in the mid-to-upper 80s and water near 80. The leeward beaches are reliably sunny, and south-shore surf picks up modestly while the north shore lies down. This is also the muggiest stretch when trade winds stall or Kona winds bring humidity from the south. Hurricane season is active in the background, peaking August–October, so watch forecasts.

October–December: the turn back to winter

October often keeps summer's dry warmth with fewer crowds, making it a favorite month. Rain and surf build again through November and into December, which is statistically the wettest month. The first whales arrive in November, and by December the season is underway. Temperatures ease back toward the low 80s on the coast. Holiday-period crowds and rates climb sharply in late December.

Wind, humidity, and what to pack

Trade winds from the northeast are the island's default and keep things comfortable; when they die and Kona winds take over, it feels hotter and stickier, and vog can settle in. Afternoons are usually windier than mornings, which is one reason to snorkel early. Pack layers for elevation and evenings, especially if you are heading up Haleakalā. Before you go

  • Rain and surf vary enormously by shore. A stormy island forecast can mean a wet Hāna and a dry Wailea the same afternoon, so check conditions where you are.
  • Winter high surf on north- and west-facing shores brings dangerous currents and shorebreak. Read live conditions and follow lifeguards before entering the water.
  • Central Pacific hurricane season (June–November, peak August–October) can bring high surf and heavy rain even from a distant storm. Monitor official forecasts.
