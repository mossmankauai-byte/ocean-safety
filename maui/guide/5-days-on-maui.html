5 Days on Maui: The Full-Island Itinerary

Five days is the sweet spot for a first Maui trip. You can do both signature outings, spend real time in the water, and still have a day that is nothing but a beach and a book. This plan keeps daily drives reasonable and leaves margin for weather and jet lag.

Quick facts

  • Five days lets you do both Haleakalā and the Road to Hāna without stacking them back-to-back.
  • One base usually beats splitting for five nights; West and South Maui are each about 45–75 minutes from the airport.
  • Haleakalā sunrise needs a recreation.gov reservation; sunset and midday do not.
  • Waiʻānapanapa State Park (the black-sand beach on the Hāna road) requires a separate advance reservation for non-residents.
  • Whale-watch boats run roughly December through April, with peak activity in January–March.

The shape of five days

With five days you can fit the two anchor trips that a shorter visit forces you to choose between: a Haleakalā sunrise and the Road to Hāna. Space them out. Put easy beach and snorkel days between the big ones so you are not driving hard two days running. If your flights cost you the first afternoon and last morning, you still have three and a half usable days.

Should you split where you stay

For five nights, most people are happier in one base than packing up midway. West Maui and South Maui both work, and each is roughly 45–75 minutes from the airport and Haleakalā. A split (say two nights near Hāna, three on the west or south side) suits people who want to slow down on the east side, but it costs you a repacking day. See first-time basics before deciding.

Days 1–2: settle in and get in the water

Arrive, stock the fridge, and start slow on a calm beach like Kamaʻole III in Kīhei or Kāʻanapali on the west side. Use day two for morning snorkeling, when the water is usually flattest, at Kapalua Bay or on a Molokini boat trip. Check the conditions hub each morning and read the snorkel safety guide first.

Day 3: Haleakalā

Book a sunrise reservation ahead through recreation.gov, or skip the 3 a.m. alarm and go for sunset or midday instead, which need no timed reservation. The summit sits above 10,000 feet and can be near freezing with strong wind even in summer, so pack a real jacket. Full details are in the sunrise reservation guide. Spend the rest of the day upcountry in Kula or Makawao, then rest.

Day 4: the Road to Hāna

Start at dawn with a full tank, food, and downloaded maps, since cell service drops out for long stretches (see the connectivity guide). The drive is about 2.5–3 hours each way over 620 curves and 59 bridges, 46 of them one lane. Waiʻānapanapa State Park with its black-sand beach requires a separate advance reservation for out-of-state visitors. Turn around with enough daylight to get back; the road is much harder in the dark.

Day 5: whales, turtles, or nothing at all

Save the last full day for something easy and weather-flexible. In winter (roughly December through April) a whale-watch boat is the standout; see the whale-watching guide. Year-round you have a good chance of seeing honu resting on the sand; the sea turtle guide explains where and the legal distance to keep. Otherwise, pick your favorite beach from the week and do nothing.

Building in weather margin

Maui's east and up-mountain areas can be rainy while the coast stays dry, and winter brings the occasional storm. Keep at least one flexible day so you can swap a wet Hāna morning for a sunny south-shore one. The month-by-month weather guide and packing list help you plan around it.

Before you go

  • Drive the Road to Hāna during daylight only. The one-lane bridges, blind curves, and cliff edges are far more dangerous after dark or in heavy rain.
  • Ocean conditions change by beach, shore, and hour. Check live conditions and follow lifeguards rather than assuming a calm-looking bay is safe.
  • The Haleakalā summit can be near freezing with high wind year-round. People underdress for it and get genuinely cold; bring a warm layer even in July.
