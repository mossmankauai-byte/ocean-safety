Where to See Sea Turtles on Maui (Respectfully)

Green sea turtles, or honu, are common in Maui's shallow water, and seeing one glide past while you snorkel is a highlight for most visitors. They are also a protected species, and the rules about keeping your distance are real laws with real fines. Here is where to find them and how to watch without causing harm.

Quick facts

  • Green sea turtles (honu) are the common species; hawksbills (honuʻea) are rare and critically endangered.
  • Hoʻokipa Beach near Paia is the most reliable spot for turtles resting on the sand, often 3–5 PM.
  • Turtles are seen in the water at Napili, Kapalua, and the Kīhei and Wailea reefs.
  • NOAA and state guidance: stay at least 10 feet (3 meters) away on land and in the water.
  • Honu are protected under the federal Endangered Species Act and Hawaiʻi law.
  • Touching, feeding, or chasing a turtle is illegal and can bring steep fines.

The two turtles you might see

Most turtles you will see are green sea turtles (honu), which come into shallow reef flats to feed on algae and sometimes haul out on beaches to rest. Less common is the hawksbill (honuʻea), which is far rarer and critically endangered. Both are protected. You do not need to know which is which to give it space, but the honu is the one resting on the sand at Hoʻokipa most afternoons.

Hoʻokipa Beach for turtles on the sand

Hoʻokipa, just east of Paia on the north shore, is the most dependable place to see honu resting on the beach, usually in the late afternoon around 3 to 5 PM near the rock wall at the east end. Volunteers often rope off the resting turtles. Watch from behind the rope, keep your distance, and do not walk up for a photo. The water here is rough and current-prone, so this is a viewing stop, not a swim. See Hoʻokipa conditions.

In the water while snorkeling

Turtles feed and rest around many Maui reefs. Napili Bay, Kapalua Bay, the Kīhei and Wailea reefs, and calmer stretches of the west side are all places snorkelers regularly encounter them. If you are in the water and a turtle swims toward you, float still and let it pass rather than chasing it. Read snorkel safety before you get in, since the best turtle reefs still have currents.

The distance rule and the law

NOAA and Hawaiʻi state guidance is to stay at least 10 feet, about 3 meters, from a turtle whether on land or in the water. Never touch, feed, chase, or ride one. Honu are protected under the federal Endangered Species Act and Hawaiʻi law, and harassment can bring fines reported up to $100,000. Touching a turtle can also transfer harmful bacteria and stress the animal, so hands off is for its sake as much as yours.

Watching the right way

Use a zoom or just enjoy it with your eyes instead of pushing closer for a shot. Do not block a turtle's path to the surface, since it needs to breathe. Keep dogs away from resting turtles on the beach. If you see someone harassing a turtle, you can report it to NOAA. Reef-friendly habits help too, so use reef-safe sunscreen to protect the reefs they feed on.

Before you go

  • Stay at least 10 feet from turtles and never touch or feed them. Harassment is a crime with fines reported up to $100,000.
  • Do not block a turtle's path to the surface; it needs air. Float still and let it swim past you.
  • Hoʻokipa's water is rough and current-prone. Treat it as a viewing beach, not a swimming one.
