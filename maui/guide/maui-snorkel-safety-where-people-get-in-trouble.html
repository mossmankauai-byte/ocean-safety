Snorkel Safety on Maui: Where People Get in Trouble

Snorkeling is the reason many people come to Maui, and it is also how visitors most often die here. Maui has led the state in snorkeling fatalities, and most of the people who drown are visitors, often on the first day or two of the trip. The water can look calm and still be dangerous. This is where people get in trouble and how to lower your odds.

Quick facts

  • Maui has led Hawaiʻi in snorkeling fatalities, and most victims are visitors.
  • Many deaths involve rapid onset pulmonary edema, which can occur in calm water without a visible struggle.
  • Black Rock at Kaʻanapali has strong currents, no lifeguards, and a high number of drownings.
  • Honolua Bay develops strong currents and a hazardous boulder entry when surf is up.
  • Currents strengthen once you leave the sheltered part of a bay; swim parallel to shore to escape one.
  • Test your gear in standing-depth water first, and consider a flotation vest if you are not a strong swimmer.

Why snorkeling turns deadly

Many snorkeling deaths are not classic drownings from rough water. Researchers studying Hawaiʻi's cases point to a pattern where snorkelers, often new to it, struggle to breathe through the mask and snorkel and quietly develop fluid in the lungs, a condition called rapid onset pulmonary edema. It can happen in calm water, without a dramatic struggle, which is part of why bystanders often do not notice until it is too late. Age and heart or lung conditions raise the risk.

Test your gear before you go far

Get comfortable with your mask and snorkel in shallow water where you can stand before heading over the reef. Make sure the mask seals and you can breathe easily without gulping or panicking. Ill-fitting rental gear is a common problem. If breathing feels like work, stop, stand up, and fix it. Full-face snorkel masks have been questioned in some of these cases, so many locals prefer a traditional mask and snorkel you can clear.

The spots with a reputation

Black Rock at Kaʻanapali is popular and has some of the most snorkel drownings in the state, with strong currents off the north side of the point and no lifeguards. Honolua Bay is calm and stunning in summer but gets strong currents that pull you toward the exposed outer reef when surf is up, plus a slippery boulder entry. Both look inviting and both catch people out. Check Kaʻanapali and Honolua Bay conditions first, and see the dangerous beaches overview.

Currents and how to handle one

Rip and channel currents are the thing that turns a nice swim into an emergency. Once you leave the sheltered part of a bay, the current can strengthen fast. If you get pulled out, do not fight straight back against it. Stay calm, keep your face out of the water enough to breathe, wave for help, and swim parallel to shore to get out of the current before heading in. Getting tired and panicked is what drowns people, not the distance itself.

Simple rules that keep you alive

Never snorkel alone, and actually keep an eye on your buddy, not just near them. Pick beaches with lifeguards when you can, and ask them about that day's conditions. Go in the morning before the wind picks up. Do not snorkel right after a big meal, alcohol, or a red-eye flight when you are exhausted. If you have a heart or lung condition or you are not a confident swimmer, wear a flotation vest, which many rental shops provide. Check the shore's surf season so you pick a coast that is calm.

When to just stay out

If waves are breaking where you want to enter, if you see whitewater or a churning brown line offshore, or if a lifeguard says no, do not go. There is no snorkel worth your life, and conditions that a local free-diver handles fine can drown a visitor. On a rough day, walk the beach, watch for turtles, and try again when the water settles.

Before you go

  • Calm-looking water is not safe water. Snorkelers drown on Maui in flat conditions, often on day one of a trip.
  • If breathing through your mask feels like effort, stop and stand up. Do not push on over deep water hoping it eases.
  • If a current pulls you out, do not fight it head-on. Stay calm, signal for help, and swim parallel to shore. Panic and exhaustion are what drown people.
  • No beach water is ever safe by default. Defer to lifeguards and live conditions, and stay out when in doubt.
