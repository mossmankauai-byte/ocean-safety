3 Days on Maui: An Honest First-Timer Itinerary

Three days is enough to fall for Maui and not enough to see it. The island is bigger and slower than it looks on a map, so the trick is to pick two or three things and do them well instead of chasing a checklist. This plan assumes you land midday on day one and fly out the morning of day four.

Quick facts

  • Pick one big day-trip: Haleakalā sunrise OR the Road to Hāna, not both.
  • Haleakalā sunrise entry (3–7 a.m.) needs a recreation.gov reservation booked ahead, plus the $30 per-vehicle park fee.
  • The Road to Hāna is a 10–12 hour round trip with stops; the drive alone is about 2.5–3 hours each way.
  • Base in West Maui or South Maui to keep daily drives short.
  • Most of historic Lahaina Front Street remains closed post-fire; nearby Kāʻanapali and Kapalua are fully open.

How to think about three days

Maui rewards people who stay in one region and take short trips out, not people who cross the island twice a day. Driving from Kāʻanapali on the west side to Hāna on the east is a full day by itself. Choose a home base, build each day around one anchor activity, and leave the afternoons loose.

On a short trip you have to pick. The two big single-day commitments, a Haleakalā sunrise and the Road to Hāna, both eat most of a day and cannot really be combined. Do one this trip and save the other.

Day 1: land, settle, easy beach

Do not schedule anything hard the day you arrive. Pick up the rental car, get groceries and water, and walk onto a calm beach in the late afternoon. If you are on the west side, Kāʻanapali or Napili Bay are easy first stops. In South Maui, the Kamaʻole beaches in Kīhei work well. Check today's conditions before you get in, and watch the lifeguard flags rather than the crowd.

Day 2: sunrise volcano or the Hāna road

This is your big day, and you choose one. Haleakalā sunrise means a 3 a.m. alarm, near-freezing air at the 10,000-foot summit, and a reservation you must book ahead through recreation.gov (see the sunrise reservation guide). The Road to Hāna is the other option, a slow 10-to-12-hour loop of waterfalls, one-lane bridges, and 600-plus curves. Both are worth it. Neither should be rushed.

Day 3: snorkel, then slow down

Spend the morning in the water while it is calm, since wind and swell usually build through the day. Molokini snorkel tours leave early from Māʻalaea; shore snorkeling is good at Kapalua Bay and, in summer, Honolua Bay. Read the snorkel safety guide first, because most Maui ocean trouble happens to snorkelers who misjudge conditions. Keep the afternoon for a slow lunch and packing.

Where to base yourself

For a first trip, West Maui (Kāʻanapali, Kahana, Kapalua) or South Maui (Kīhei, Wailea) both make sense. Both put you near calm beaches, restaurants, and snorkel boats. South Maui is a bit more central for Haleakalā and the airport; West Maui is closer to whale-watching waters in winter. See what to know for a first trip for the fuller rundown.

What to skip on a short trip

Do not try to do Haleakalā and Hāna on back-to-back days; you will be exhausted and driving tired on dangerous roads. Skip the far-flung stuff, like the back road past Hāna or a Lānaʻi ferry day. On West Maui, plan around Lahaina: most of historic Front Street is still a closed rebuilding zone after the 2023 fire, so treat the town with care and stick to the businesses that have reopened rather than the fire site.

Before you go

  • Never judge ocean safety by how calm it looks or how many people are in the water. Check live conditions and defer to lifeguards and posted flags every time.
  • A Haleakalā sunrise means a pre-dawn drive down a steep mountain road afterward while tired. Bring a warm layer, and pull over if you are drowsy.
  • On West Maui, do not treat the Lahaina fire zone as a sightseeing stop. Much of it is a closed construction area and a place people lost their homes.
