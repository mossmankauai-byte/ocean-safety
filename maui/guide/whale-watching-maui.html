Whale Watching on Maui: Season, Where, and How

Every winter, thousands of humpback whales swim from Alaska to the warm, shallow water between Maui, Lānaʻi, Molokaʻi, and Kahoʻolawe to mate, give birth, and raise calves. Maui is one of the best places on Earth to see them, and for a stretch of the year you can spot breaches and spouts from shore without paying for anything. Here is when they come, where to look, and the rules that protect them.

Quick facts

  • Humpbacks are in Maui waters roughly November through April; peak is January through March.
  • The ʻAuʻau Channel between Maui, Lānaʻi, and Molokaʻi is the main nursery.
  • You can watch for free from highway pullouts and the Kāʻanapali, Wailea, and Kīhei coasts.
  • Boat tours leave from Māʻalaea and Lahaina Harbor, many with peak-season sighting guarantees.
  • Federal law requires staying 100 yards (300 feet) from humpbacks; drones must stay 1,000 feet up.
  • Humpbacks are federally protected inside the Hawaiian Islands Humpback Whale National Marine Sanctuary.

When to go

Humpbacks start arriving as early as November and some linger into April or May. The reliable window is January through March, when the most whales are here and mother-calf pairs, breaching, and pec-slapping are at their peak. If your trip falls outside those months, you may still get lucky on the shoulders, but December and early April are hit or miss. Watching from shore for free

You do not need a boat. The elevated pullouts along the Honoapiʻilani Highway between Māʻalaea and Lahaina, the beaches at Wailea and Kīhei, and the coast around Kāʻanapali all give good sightlines over the whale nursery in the ʻAuʻau Channel. Bring binoculars, look for the puff of a spout, then keep watching that spot. Morning tends to have calmer wind and easier water reading. Whales can appear right off Kaʻanapali and Wailea.

Taking a boat tour

Boat tours run out of Māʻalaea and Lahaina Harbor and get you closer to the action, often with a naturalist and a hydrophone to hear whale song. Many operators offer a sighting guarantee during peak season, meaning a free re-ride if no whales show. Smaller rafts feel more up-close but are a rougher, wetter ride, while larger catamarans are steadier and better if anyone gets seasick. Book ahead in January and February.

The 100-yard rule

Federal law requires boats, swimmers, kayakers, and paddlers to stay at least 100 yards, about 300 feet, from any humpback in Hawaiian waters. Drones and aircraft must stay 1,000 feet above them. If a whale approaches you, the rule still applies, so the operator should put engines in neutral and let it pass. These are endangered animals protected under the National Marine Sanctuary and federal law, and the distance is not a suggestion.

What you are actually seeing

The spout is the whale exhaling. A breach is a full-body leap, still not fully explained by scientists. Pec slaps and tail slaps may be communication. The eerie songs, which you can sometimes hear through the hull, are sung mostly by males. Calves are born here at around 10 to 15 feet and nurse for months before the long swim back north. You are watching a nursery, so give mothers and calves extra room.

Before you go

  • Keep 100 yards from whales in the water and by boat. If one surfaces close, hold still and let it move on rather than swimming toward it.
  • Winter is also big-surf season on the north and west shores. Pick safe, stable footing for shore watching and mind the waves.
  • Do not fly a drone near whales. Aircraft, including drones, must stay 1,000 feet above them by law.
