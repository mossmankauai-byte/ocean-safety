Maui's most dangerous beaches

Maui is beautiful and unforgiving. These 20 beaches have the strongest shorebreak, currents or drowning history on the island — most have no lifeguard. Read the water, and when in doubt, choose a guarded beach.

Puʻu Kekaʻa (Black Rock) — West Maui
Strong current wraps around the lava point and offshore; this is one of Hawaiʻi's highest snorkel-drowning locations. The popular cliff jump at the tip has caused drownings and serious injuries. No lifeguard. Snorkel only inside the cove on calm days, stay close to shore, and do not jump from the rock.
Honolua Bay — West Maui
Faces NNW and takes the full force of winter N/NW swell, turning into a powerful big-wave surf break (Oct–Apr) with no lifeguard, strong currents and a rocky/boulder entry. Snorkel here only on calm flat summer days; in winter stay out of the water and watch from the cliff.
Mokuleʻia Beach (Slaughterhouse) — West Maui
NNW-facing pocket beach in the Honolua–Mokuleʻia Marine Life Conservation District with no lifeguard, steep cliff stairway access and a heavy, dumping shorebreak plus rip currents on any north/NW swell (most of winter). Enter only on flat calm summer days; rescues here are difficult.
Keawakapu Beach — South Maui
No lifeguard on a long, open beach with documented snorkeling fatalities (a visitor snorkeler drowned here in Aug 2017) and rip currents that strengthen on south swells, especially toward the rocky ends and offshore. Midday trade winds pick up and can push swimmers and snorkelers out. Snorkel only on a calm morning, stay close to shore, never snorkel alone, and judge the surf yourself — there is no one watching the water.
Mokapu & Ulua Beach — South Maui
No lifeguard. A 63-year-old visitor drowned while snorkeling off Mokapu in July 2018, part of a documented pattern of snorkeling deaths on Maui's unguarded south-shore reefs. The popular reef point looks calm in the morning but afternoon trade winds (typically by ~2pm) build chop and current, and water can pull offshore. Snorkel only on a calm morning, stay close to shore, never snorkel alone, and get out when the wind comes up.
Wailea Beach — South Maui
No lifeguard on a beach with a serious recent drowning history. In May 2025 two visitors (a father and son) died in rip-current conditions off Wailea Beach; bystanders, not lifeguards, attempted the rescue. A snorkeler also died here in 2022, and rip-current rescues have recurred over the years. The water looks calm and resort-tame but rip currents form, especially toward the south point and on south swells. Swim or snorkel only when genuinely calm, stay close to shore, never go alone, and if caught in a current don't fight it — float and signal for help.
Polo Beach — South Maui
No lifeguard, on the same Wailea resort coast where multiple visitor drownings have occurred at adjacent unguarded beaches (Wailea Beach, Mokapu). Shorebreak and rip currents pick up on south swells, and there is no one watching the water. Swim or snorkel only when calm, stay close to shore, and never go alone.
Maluaka Beach — South Maui
No lifeguard. A 71-year-old visitor drowned while snorkeling alone off Maluaka ('Turtle Town') in Jan 2018. The reef draws crowds of snorkelers but the water can develop current and chop, and people venture far offshore chasing turtles. Snorkel only on a calm morning, stay close to shore, never snorkel alone, and turn back when the wind builds. Nearest lifeguard is at Makena State Park (Big Beach), not here.
Mākena Landing — South Maui
No lifeguard and NOT a sandy swim beach — entry is over lava rock, so this is a shore-dive and kayak launch for confident swimmers only. It sits within the Mākena 'Turtle Town' reef system, where a visitor snorkeler drowned in Jan 2018; snorkelers here range far offshore toward the lava points and can be caught by current and afternoon wind. Use water shoes, snorkel only on a flat calm morning, stay with a buddy, and turn back when conditions build.
Big Beach (Oneloa, Mākena State Park) — South Maui
Powerful dumping shorebreak that breaks directly onto a steep sand face with NO trough — it can drive swimmers headfirst into the sand. Mākena/Big Beach LEADS the State of Hawaiʻi in spinal-cord injuries (Hawaiʻi Trauma Registry recorded 40 spinal injuries in the Mākena area, 33 at Big Beach, 2009–2017), with paralyzing neck and back injuries, plus broken bones and drownings. Lifeguards are present 8am–4:45pm but CANNOT prevent the shorebreak itself. Stay out of the water unless it is genuinely flat and you have strong ocean experience; never turn your back on the waves.
Little Beach (Puʻu ʻŌlaʻi) — South Maui
No lifeguard and no vehicle access — reached only by a short, steep, slippery scramble over the lava bluff from the north end of Big Beach. Shorebreak and a strong offshore current can pound and pull swimmers; entry/exit is over rocks. Bodysurfers ride it, but it is not a casual swim beach. Note: the cove is an unofficial clothing-optional spot and public nudity is illegal on Maui — signage now prohibits nudity, alcohol and fires.
H.A. Baldwin Beach Park — North Shore
Lifeguarded, but the open main beach has a hard, dumping shorebreak that breaks directly on sand — a documented neck/spinal-injury hotspot ('there's a reason locals joke about a good chiropractor in Paia'). Do NOT bodysurf or stand in the shorebreak. Keep keiki only in the calm reef-protected 'Baby Beach' cove at the far west end (or 'Baldwin Cove' at the east end), and always check with the lifeguard about conditions before entering.
Tavares Beach — North Shore
No lifeguard and a DOCUMENTED DROWNING: a 69-year-old man was found dead face-down in the Tavares Bay shorebreak (Aug 2013). The reef, currents and shorebreak change fast and there is no one watching the water. Treat as a stroll/scout-only spot — do not enter unless conditions are clearly calm and you are a strong swimmer with a buddy; never swim alone here.
Kahului Harbor Beaches (Hoʻaloha / Kanahā Pond) — North Shore
This is a working commercial/cruise harbor, NOT a swimming beach — there is no safe ocean entry here. No lifeguard, harbor and vessel traffic, stream/urban runoff and poor water clarity. Highest water-quality advisory frequency on the island after rain (wait 72+ hours). Use it only as an in-town leg-stretch/photo stop; do not swim or snorkel.
Hoʻokipa Beach Park — North Shore
Expert-only water. A rock and reef ledge rings a tiny pocket of sand, with strong rip currents, urchins, and heavy shorebreak — even with lifeguards present, swimming is not recommended. World-class winter waves (over ~20 ft on N swells) draw pro windsurfers and surfers only. Sudden building afternoon winds and shallow reef can quickly overpower novices; rogue waves possible even on calm-looking days. Watch the action from the bluff; do not enter unless you are a local-level wave sailor and very strong swimmer.
Sugar Cove Beach — North Shore
No lifeguard and fully open to north-shore swell. In any north or NE swell — most common October through April — this pocket gets pounded by surf and a reef-edge current that can pull you offshore; the 4-year record shows it is rough roughly one day in seven. Treat it as a walk-and-watch beach: only strong swimmers should enter on a calm, small-surf day, and check the surf forecast first. Winter boogie-boarding here is for experienced ocean-goers only.
Hāmoa Beach — East/Hāna
Open-ocean pocket beach with no reef and no lifeguard. Documented strong rip current that pulls swimmers toward the rocks at the ends, plus a hard dumping shorebreak on a swell. Hāmoa is repeatedly named among Maui's risky beaches for rescues. Enter only on a genuinely flat, calm day, stay close to shore, and never turn your back on the water — when in doubt, stay out.
Koki Beach — East/Hāna
Persistent strong rip currents and a steep, dumping shorebreak on red/dark sand, fully exposed to open-ocean swell. No lifeguard. Multiple documented drownings (a man in Dec 2012; a swimmer in March 2022 pulled out and lost even in 'calm' conditions). This is NOT a swimming beach — stay on the sand.
Waiʻānapanapa Black Sand Beach (Paʻiloa) — East/Hāna
No protective reef, so the water deepens abruptly at the shoreline and a powerful, violent shorebreak funnels into a small lava-rock cove with strong currents and rip. No lifeguard. Swimmers have been swept onto the rocks. Portuguese man o' war are sometimes present. Treat as look-don't-touch; enter only on a genuinely flat day, if at all.
Kaihalulu (Red Sand Beach) — East/Hāna
ACCESS IS THE PRIMARY KILLER: the only way in is a narrow, actively eroding cinder-cliff trail across a steep dropoff onto loose rock. At least a dozen serious falls since 2012, multiple helicopter airlifts (2023 and 2024), and the trail has been closed/advised-against after landslides and the 2026 Kona storms. The route crosses private land and Hawaiian burial grounds. The cove has a rock-gap opening to the open ocean where surge and rip can pull swimmers onto the reef. No lifeguard, no rescue access. Local groups ask visitors to stay away. Do not attempt.
