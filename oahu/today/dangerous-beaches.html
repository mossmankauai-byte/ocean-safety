Oʻahu's most dangerous beaches

These 17 beaches have the strongest shorebreak, currents or drowning history on Oʻahu — most have no lifeguard. When in doubt, choose a guarded beach.

Waimea Bay — North Shore
Summer it can be calm and swimmable, but from roughly Oct–Apr Waimea sees giant NW swell, a violent sand-bottom shorebreak that breaks necks, and strong rip currents. Never enter on a high-surf day. Jumping off the rock has killed and paralyzed people. Always check with the lifeguard tower.
ʻEhukai Beach (Banzai Pipeline) — North Shore
Pipeline/Backdoor breaks over a shallow, jagged reef just feet from shore. Even pro surfers die and are seriously injured here. The shorebreak and reef make this one of the deadliest surf spots in the world. Watch from the sand — non-experts must stay out of the water in any surf.
Sunset Beach — North Shore
Notorious for powerful, fast-moving rip currents (the ‘Sunset rip’) and large winter NW surf with hard shorebreak. Frequent rescues and drownings. Calm-looking days can still hide strong currents; check with lifeguards before entering and never swim in surf.
Sharks Cove (Pūpūkea) — North Shore
Part of the Pūpūkea Marine Life Conservation District — superb summer snorkeling, but the sharp lava-rock entry and exposure mean even moderate swell turns it deadly. Drownings and serious injuries occur when people enter on surf days (bodies recovered during high surf as recently as Dec 2023). Flat, calm summer mornings only; check the lifeguard and never enter with any wave action.
Laniākea Beach — North Shore
Treat as no lifeguard. Reef and rock bottom plus strong winter currents (a strong rip forms in the west-side channel on swells) make swimming hazardous; it is a turtle-viewing beach, not a safe swim beach. Stay well back from basking honu (state and federal law) and beware fast traffic when crossing the highway from parking.
Three Tables — North Shore
Part of the Pūpūkea MLCD with no dedicated lifeguard at this spot. Shallow reef tables and a rocky entry make it dangerous in any swell — Oct–Apr north swells push 8–20ft surf into the bay and turn the entry into a washing machine; people drown here every winter. Flat, calm summer conditions only (roughly May–Sep). If white water is breaking on the outer reef, stay on the sand.
Chun's Reef — North Shore
Treat as no lifeguard and a shallow reef bottom. Often the ‘mellow’ North Shore option, but winter swell still produces strong currents and the reef causes injuries. Only suitable for confident swimmers/surfers in small, calm conditions.
Lanikai Beach — Windward (East)
The shoreline itself is calm and reef-shielded, BUT there is no lifeguard and the water off Lanikai toward the Mokulua islets is deadly. At least four people have drowned in the waters off the Mokuluas since 1993, and in January 2024 Honolulu Ocean Safety pulled an unresponsive kayaker from the water roughly 100 yards off the beach and performed CPR. The channel and the swells that wrap around and clash between the two islands generate strong, deceptive currents that flip kayaks and exhaust swimmers; the Mokes are out of sight of the Kailua Beach lifeguard towers, so no one is watching and rescue is slow. Do not paddle, swim, or kayak to the Mokes without a PFD, strong open-water ability, and settled conditions.
Makapuʻu Beach Park — Windward (East)
Powerful, steep dumping shorebreak with no protecting reef — one of Oʻahu's most injury-prone bodyboard beaches. The wave breaks in inches of water directly onto sand and causes neck, back and shoulder (cervical-spine) injuries every year; strong rip currents pull out on bigger days. Guarded, but for expert bodyboarders/bodysurfers only — inexperienced swimmers and visitors should watch from shore. The city posts shorebreak warning signs at the access.
Hanauma Bay — South Shore (Waikīkī)
World-class snorkeling on the protected inner reef, but powerful rip currents run through the reef channels and out the bay mouth — at least ~51 drowning deaths recorded here since 1995. The 'Witch's Brew' cove, the back-reef toward the Toilet Bowl, and the cable-channel/Molokai Express current pull swimmers past the reef into the open bay. Stay inside the inner reef flat, never swim out over or past the reef, snorkel with a buddy, and obey the lifeguards' flagged areas.
Sandy Beach — South Shore (Waikīkī)
The most dangerous shorebreak on Oʻahu and reportedly the highest neck/back-injury beach in the United States ('Break Neck Beach'). A steep sand-bottom drop-off forces waves to pitch and slam riders headfirst onto hard-packed sand — most spinal injuries happen on SMALL days, not big ones, and rip currents and undertow appear as surf builds. Lifeguards make rescues at roughly 2.5x/day and treat every rescue as a potential spinal injury. Only experienced bodysurfers/bodyboarders should enter, and only after checking with lifeguards. Visitors should stay out of the water.
China Walls (Koko Kai) — South Shore (Waikīkī)
A lava cliff shelf, NOT a swimming beach, and one of the most dangerous spots on Oʻahu — rescues happen frequently, especially in the April–October south-swell season. Large sets sweep without warning along the rock and pull people off the ledge into deep water against the cliff, with no easy exit and no lifeguard. Cliff-jumpers are routinely slammed on the rocks and several people have drowned. Honolulu Ocean Safety urges people not to swim or jump here.
Mākaha Beach — Leeward (West)
Powerful surf beach. Calm and swimmable only on flat summer days; west and northwest swells (mainly fall–winter, waves to 25ft off Makaha Point) build a strong dumping shorebreak, heavy backwash, rip currents, and a shallow reef. Documented rescues and drownings on the Makaha–Pokai stretch. Lifeguarded (Towers 47A/47B) — never enter without checking with the guards, and only swim directly in front of a staffed tower.
Māʻili Beach Park — Leeward (West)
Long open west-facing beach exposed to west/northwest swell. Shifting sandbars and a sharp dumping shorebreak when swell is up, with rip currents; drownings are documented along the Maili stretch of the Waianae coast. Two lifeguard towers (43A/43B) — only swim directly in front of a staffed tower and ask about the day's current before entering.
Nānākuli Beach Park — Leeward (West)
Strong shorebreak that can dump even on summer days, and high surf with strong currents in winter. Drownings are documented on the Nanakuli stretch of the Waianae coast. Lifeguarded (Tower 41, Kalanianaʻole Beach Park) — swim only directly in front of a staffed tower and check conditions first; snorkel only on flat, calm days.
Electric Beach (Kahe Point) — Leeward (West)
Snorkel-only site with NO safe swim entry: a rocky lava-bench shore and an unavoidable ~200-yard open-water swim out to the power-plant outflow pipes, with real currents. A snorkeler drowned here in 2023 (before the tower existed) and it is a recognized rescue hot spot; tour groups concentrate inexperienced snorkelers here. A lifeguard tower now exists but the outflow swim is well offshore — go ONLY on flat, calm days, with a buddy and a dive flag, and check in with the lifeguard first.
Yokohama Bay (Keawaʻula) — Leeward (West)
Remote beach with a steep, dumping shorebreak, strong currents, and a rocky bottom. There is a lifeguard tower (Tower 50) at the very end of the road, but it is frequently understaffed or unstaffed and you must not count on a guard being present. Multiple deaths have occurred in this area, including two 17-year-old fishermen swept off the rocks near here in October 2025. Enter only on a flat, calm summer day, near a staffed tower; in any swell, stay on the sand. Treat surrounding rocky points as no-go in any surf.
