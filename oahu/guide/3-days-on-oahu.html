3 Days on Oʻahu: An Honest First-Timer Itinerary

Three days is enough to see the best of Oʻahu if you stop trying to do the whole island every day. This plan groups each day by region so you spend your time at beaches and trailheads instead of stuck on the H-1. It assumes a rental car for at least two of the three days, and it flags the two spots you must reserve before you arrive.

Quick facts

  • Group each day by region (south shore, windward, North Shore) to avoid backtracking across the island.
  • Diamond Head needs an out-of-state reservation (Go Wild system, up to 30 days ahead): $5 entry, $10 parking, credit card only.
  • Hanauma Bay is reservation-only, closed Monday and Tuesday; tickets release two days ahead at 7:00 a.m. HST and sell out fast. Non-resident admission is $25.
  • North Shore beaches are usually calm in summer but produce large, dangerous surf from about November through March.
  • Waikiki has lifeguards and is the gentlest water for a first day, but still has rip currents and shore break.
  • Lanikai parking is residential and enforced; arrive early.

Day 1: Waikiki and the south shore

Start with Diamond Head while it is cool and the light is good. Out-of-state visitors need a reservation, and it books up, so lock it in ahead of time (details below). The hike is short but steep with stairs and a tunnel; budget an hour up and back plus time at the crater rim.

Spend the afternoon on Waikiki Beach, which is the gentlest introduction to Oʻahu's water and has lifeguards along most of its length. It still has rip currents, shore break, and boat traffic, so check the flags and the live Waikiki conditions before you get in. If you want a quieter stretch, walk toward Kaimana Beach at the Diamond Head end.

Day 2: The windward (east) side

Drive over the Pali to the windward coast. Kailua Beach Park and Lanikai are the draw here: long, soft sand and, on calm days, some of the clearer water on the island. Parking in the Lanikai neighborhood is tight, residential, and enforced, so arrive early and read the signs. Check Lanikai and Kailua Beach Park conditions first, because the windward side gets wind and chop that Waikiki does not.

On the way back, stop at the Makapuʻu Point lighthouse trail for the coastal view, and watch for turtles and, in winter, whales offshore. See our sea turtle guide for where they show up.

Day 3: The North Shore (with a seasonal caveat)

The North Shore is Haleʻiwa town, food trucks, and the big-name beaches at Waimea, ʻEhukai (Pipeline), and Sunset. It is about an hour from Waikiki. In summer these beaches are usually calm enough to swim and snorkel. From roughly November through March they are not: the same breaks that draw pro surfers produce large, dangerous surf and strong currents, and people drown here every winter.

Treat the water as a spectator sport in winter. Watch from the sand, stay well back from the shore break, and follow the lifeguards and posted warnings. Check Waimea Bay and ʻEhukai/Pipeline before you plan on getting in, and read our surf season guide to know which shore is calm when you visit.

Reservations to book before you arrive

Two spots need advance booking. Diamond Head requires a reservation for out-of-state visitors, made through the state's Go Wild system up to 30 days ahead; entry is $5 per person and parking is $10 per vehicle, credit card only. Hanauma Bay, if you want to snorkel there, is reservation-only, closed Monday and Tuesday, and tickets release two days ahead at 7:00 a.m. Hawaiʻi time and sell out within minutes. Non-resident admission is $25, kids 12 and under free.

If you do not have a Hanauma reservation, do not build your day around it. There are easier snorkel and beach options that need no booking.

If you would rather not drive

You can do Day 1 entirely on foot or by short rideshare from Waikiki. For the windward and North Shore days, TheBus reaches Kailua and Haleʻiwa but turns a one-hour drive into a much longer trip, and connections are limited. If you are carless for all three days, see getting around Oʻahu without a car and expect to lean on tours or rideshare for the far corners of the island.

How to adjust for weather

Oʻahu's leeward south shore, where Waikiki sits, is the drier, sunnier side. The windward and North Shore catch more rain, especially in winter. If a wet system rolls through on your windward or North Shore day, flip the order and save that region for a clearer one. Our driving times guide helps you avoid the worst of the commute-hour traffic when you move between regions.

Before you go

  • North Shore beaches (Waimea, Pipeline, Sunset) have deadly surf and currents in winter. Watch from the sand, obey lifeguards, and do not enter the water when the surf is up.
  • Even calm-looking beaches have rip currents and shore break. Check the daily flags and live conditions, and swim where there are lifeguards.
  • Do not build a day around Hanauma Bay unless you already hold a reservation. It is closed Monday and Tuesday and sells out within minutes of release.
