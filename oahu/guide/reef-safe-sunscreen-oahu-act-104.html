Reef-Safe Sunscreen on Oʻahu: What Act 104 Means for Your Bag

Act 104 sounds like chemistry homework, but in practice it makes packing simple. Since 2021 it has been illegal in Hawaiʻi to sell sunscreen containing oxybenzone or octinoxate without a prescription, because those chemicals damage coral. The safe move for your bag is mineral sunscreen, and how you use it matters most in the minutes before you get in the water.

Quick facts

  • Act 104 bans the sale of sunscreens with oxybenzone or octinoxate statewide, without a prescription, and took effect January 1, 2021.
  • It is a sales ban, so read active ingredients and choose a mineral formula (zinc oxide or titanium dioxide only).
  • Terms like reef safe and reef friendly are unregulated marketing; the ingredient list is what counts.
  • Hanauma Bay and other protected sites ask for mineral-only sunscreen, and lawmakers have moved to restrict more chemicals.
  • Apply mineral sunscreen 15 to 30 minutes before sun, reapply after swimming, and prefer lotions over aerosol sprays.

What Act 104 actually bans

Act 104 is a statewide law that took effect January 1, 2021. It bans the sale, offer for sale, and distribution of over-the-counter sunscreens containing oxybenzone or octinoxate anywhere in Hawaiʻi without a doctor's prescription. It targets those two chemicals specifically, which studies link to coral bleaching and harm to marine life. It is a sales ban, not a possession ban, so it governs what stores here can put on the shelf.

What it means for your bag

Because Act 104 restricts sales rather than possession, you technically can bring a chemical sunscreen from the mainland. But the reef does not care where you bought the bottle, and several state-managed sites restrict what you can actually use on the beach. The simplest approach is to leave the old bottle home and pack or buy a mineral sunscreen. That way you are compliant at the register and at the water's edge.

How to read a label

Ignore the marketing on the front. Turn the bottle over and read the active ingredients. A true mineral sunscreen lists only zinc oxide, titanium dioxide, or both. If you see oxybenzone or octinoxate, it cannot be sold here and you should not use it in the water. Terms like reef safe and reef friendly are not regulated, so the ingredient list is the only thing that tells you the truth.

Where the rules go further

Some places on Oʻahu push past the statewide minimum. Hanauma Bay, a protected marine preserve, asks visitors to use only mineral (non-nano) sunscreen, and staff there actively encourage compliance. Beyond Act 104, Hawaiʻi lawmakers have moved to restrict additional sunscreen chemicals, and some state sites and retailers already go further than the two-chemical ban. Note that Maui County (a different island) has an even stricter mineral-only law; that rule does not apply on Oʻahu, but a mineral-only bottle keeps you covered on any island you visit.

Using it the right way

Mineral sunscreen works best when you apply it 15 to 30 minutes before sun exposure and reapply after swimming, sweating, or toweling off. Rub in lotions rather than using aerosol sprays, which drift onto sand and reef and waste product. Even better, cover up: a rash guard, a wide-brim hat, and shade block far more sun than any lotion, and they keep chemicals off the reef entirely.

Why it matters here

Hawaiʻi's reefs are under real stress from warming water, and daily sunscreen loads add to it at popular snorkel spots. Cutting the harmful chemicals is a small, easy thing a visitor can do that actually helps. It also keeps you on the right side of the law and out of an awkward conversation at a preserve gate. Pair this with the packing guide and check today's conditions before you snorkel.

Before you go

  • Sunscreen is a backup, not your main defense; a rash guard, hat, and shade protect you and the reef far better than lotion alone.
  • Do not trust reef safe labels at face value; a product can carry the phrase and still contain banned chemicals, so check the active ingredients.
  • Rules are tightening and vary by site; when unsure, use a mineral-only sunscreen and follow posted rules at preserves like Hanauma Bay.
