Oʻahu Surf Season: When Each Shore Is Calm (and When the North Shore Is Not)

Oʻahu's shores trade off through the year. In winter, the North Shore and West Side take huge swells while the South Shore stays mostly small. In summer, that flips: the South Shore gets its waves and the North Shore often goes flat. Knowing which side is doing what in the month you visit is the difference between an easy swim and standing in front of a wave that can hold you under.

Quick facts

  • North Shore and West Side get big, dangerous swells roughly November through February or March.
  • South Shore (Waikiki, Ala Moana) gets its smaller, cleaner surf in summer, roughly May through September.
  • North Shore winter surf can reach 20 to 40+ foot faces at Pipeline, Sunset, and Waimea; watch from shore, do not swim.
  • Windward beaches like Lanikai and Kailua are protected by offshore reefs and tend to be calmer much of the year.
  • No Oʻahu beach is calm year-round; conditions shift daily, so check live reports before entering the water.

The basic pattern

Oʻahu faces waves from different directions depending on the season. Winter storms in the North Pacific send big swells at the north- and west-facing shores from roughly November through February or March. Summer swells come from the far Southern Hemisphere and hit the south-facing shores from about May through September, but they arrive much smaller. The windward (east) side gets trade-wind chop most of the year rather than big clean swell.

The short version: winter waves live on the North Shore and West Side, summer waves are on the South Shore, and no single beach is calm all year. Always check today's conditions before you go in.

North Shore: dangerous in winter, tame in summer

From November into February, the North Shore is where the giant surf shows up. Pipeline, Sunset Beach, and Waimea Bay can see faces of 20 to 40 feet and beyond on the biggest days. These are world-class waves for experts and genuinely deadly for everyone else, with powerful shorebreak and strong currents even on medium days. Watching from the sand is the move in winter, not swimming.

In summer the same beaches often go flat or waist-high, and spots like Waimea Bay can be calm enough to swim. That calm is seasonal, not permanent, and a summer swell can still pop up. See per-beach conditions for Pipeline, Waimea Bay, and Sunset Beach before deciding.

South Shore: summer's turn

The South Shore, including Waikiki and Ala Moana, gets its swell in summer. These waves are far gentler than the North Shore's winter surf, which is why Waikiki is where most people learn to surf. Even so, summer south swells can bump up the size and current at spots like Ala Moana Bowls, and Waikiki has reef and boat channels to be aware of. In winter the South Shore is usually the calmer side of the island. Check Waikiki and Ala Moana for the day.

West Side and windward: the in-between

The West (leeward) Side, from Koʻolina up the Waiʻanae coast, catches some of the same north and west winter swells the North Shore does, so it can get big and hazardous in winter, with a few protected spots like the Koʻolina lagoons staying calmer. The windward east side, including Lanikai and Kailua, is more about trade-wind chop than big swell, and the offshore reefs at Lanikai and Kailua Beach Park knock a lot of the energy down, which is why these tend to be among the more swimmable beaches much of the year. Wind still builds afternoon chop here.

How to plan around it

Match your beach to the season. If you are visiting in winter and want to swim, favor the South Shore and the calmer windward spots, and treat the North Shore and West Side as places to watch waves rather than enter the water. In summer, the whole island is generally calmer, and the South Shore is where the small rideable surf is. Whatever the season, conditions change day to day, so read the live conditions and the list of more dangerous beaches, and follow lifeguard flags and signs. For a broader month-by-month view, see the best time to visit Oʻahu.

Before you go

  • North Shore and West Side winter surf regularly kills and injures visitors. Big-wave beaches are for watching, not swimming, and waves can reach you well up the sand.
  • A shore being calm in one season does not make it safe on a given day. Always check live conditions and follow lifeguards and posted signs.
  • Never turn your back on the ocean at a shorebreak or rocky shoreline; sets can arrive suddenly and sweep people off rocks or ledges.
