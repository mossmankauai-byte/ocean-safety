Hawaiian Monk Seals on Oʻahu: Where You Might See One and the Rules

The Hawaiian monk seal is one of the most endangered seals on Earth, and seeing one hauled out on an Oʻahu beach is genuinely special. It is not something you can plan, though; they show up where they show up. If you find one, the job is to leave it alone, keep well back, and let it rest.

Quick facts

  • Only around 1,500 to 1,600 Hawaiian monk seals exist, making them one of the world's most endangered seals.
  • They haul out to rest on beaches all over Oʻahu with no single reliable viewing spot.
  • Stay at least 50 feet away, and at least 150 feet from a mother and pup, behind any signs.
  • A seal lying still on the sand is usually just resting; do not touch it or push it into the water.
  • Report sightings, especially injured or entangled seals or those with pups, to NOAA at (888) 256-9840.

What they are

Hawaiian monk seals are found only in Hawaiʻi. The whole species numbers only around 1,500 to 1,600 animals, most in the remote Northwestern Hawaiian Islands, with a few hundred spread across the main islands. They are protected under the federal Endangered Species Act, the Marine Mammal Protection Act, and Hawaiʻi state law. After decades of decline the population has been slowly growing, but every individual still matters.

Where you might see one

Monk seals haul out to rest, molt, and warm up on sandy beaches all around Oʻahu, including quieter stretches of the North Shore, the west side, and sometimes even busy spots near town. There is no reliable viewing beach the way Laniakea works for turtles, because seals move and rest wherever they like. When one comes ashore, responders often rope off the area and post signs. If you come across a roped-off zone on the sand, a seal is likely resting inside it.

The distance rules

Give a resting monk seal room. NOAA asks people to stay at least 50 feet away, and at least 150 feet from a mother with a pup, and to stay behind any signs or barriers. A hauled-out seal that looks sick or dead is usually just resting, which is normal. Do not touch it, pour water on it, feed it, or try to push it back into the ocean. Keep dogs leashed and well away, since seals and dogs can transmit diseases to each other. Getting close is harassment of an endangered animal and is illegal.

If you find one

Keep your distance, keep quiet, and keep others and their dogs back too. Report the sighting to the NOAA Marine Wildlife Hotline at (888) 256-9840, especially if the seal is entangled, injured, or has a pup, or if a crowd is forming. Do not post the exact location publicly in a way that could draw a crowd. If volunteers are on scene, follow their guidance. The best thing you can do is admire it from far enough away that it does not react to you at all.

Seals and pups

Mother seals nurse their pups on the beach for several weeks and can be protective, so the wider 150-foot buffer is both for the animals and for your safety; a mother monk seal is large and will defend her pup. Never get between a seal and the water, and never get between a mother and her pup. If a pupping area is fenced or signed, stay out of it entirely. Monk seals share beaches with honu and migrating whales, all protected, all best watched from a distance.

Before you go

  • Stay at least 50 feet from a monk seal and 150 feet from a mother with a pup. Approaching, touching, or feeding one is illegal harassment of an endangered animal.
  • A mother seal will defend her pup and can move fast. Never get between a seal and the ocean, or between a mother and her pup.
  • Keep dogs leashed and far away; seals and dogs can pass diseases to each other. Report distressed or entangled seals to NOAA at (888) 256-9840.
