Where to See Sea Turtles on Oʻahu (Respectfully)

Green sea turtles, or honu, are common in Oʻahu's waters and regularly haul out to rest on a few North Shore beaches. Seeing one is one of the easier wildlife wins on the island. They are protected by law, though, and the rule is simple: keep your distance and never touch or feed them.

Quick facts

  • Laniakea Beach on the North Shore is Oʻahu's most reliable spot for basking green sea turtles (honu).
  • Turtles often haul out midday and are less likely to come ashore during big winter surf.
  • Hawaiʻi asks visitors to stay at least 10 feet from turtles on land and in the water.
  • Touching, feeding, chasing, or crowding turtles is illegal and can bring penalties.
  • Honu also appear at Haleʻiwa and along South Shore reefs near Waikiki and Diamond Head.

Laniakea (Turtle Beach)

Laniakea Beach on the North Shore, along Kamehameha Highway between Haleiwa and Waimea Bay, is the most reliable place to see honu. Turtles come ashore to bask, often in the midday hours, and volunteers frequently rope off resting animals and answer questions. It is roughly a 2.5-mile drive north of Haleiwa town. Turtles are less likely to haul out during big winter surf, and parking is limited across the highway, so arrive early and cross the road carefully.

Other spots to try

Honu also turn up at Haleiwa, around Haleʻiwa Aliʻi Beach Park, and in the water at snorkeling spots island-wide. On the South Shore they feed along reefs near Waikiki and Diamond Head. Any beach with reef and calm-enough water in summer can produce a sighting. You are seeing wild animals on their schedule, so no spot is guaranteed on a given day. Check today's conditions before heading out, since North Shore access is tied to the surf.

The rules and the law

Green sea turtles are protected under federal and Hawaiʻi state law. Hawaiʻi's Department of Land and Natural Resources asks people to stay at least 10 feet away from turtles on land and in the water, and NOAA's broader guidance is to give them even more room where you can. Do not touch, ride, chase, feed, or pour water on them. Do not block a turtle's path back to the sea. Getting close for a photo can count as harassment and carries real penalties, so use zoom instead of your feet.

Snorkeling near turtles

If a honu swims up while you are snorkeling, hold still or slowly back away and let it pass. Do not swim after it, reach for it, or position yourself between the turtle and the surface it needs to reach for air. Keep in mind that many turtle spots are also reef spots with sharp coral, surge, and current, so mind your footing and the conditions as much as the wildlife. Why it matters

Hawaiian green sea turtles were heavily depleted and have recovered under decades of protection, but they remain vulnerable to boat strikes, fishing gear, and human disturbance. A basking turtle is resting, warming up, and conserving energy. Crowding it forces it back into the water and undoes that. Watching quietly from 10 feet or more, and reminding others to do the same, is how the honu keep coming ashore. While you are out, you may also encounter Hawaiian monk seals, which have stricter rules.

Before you go

  • Stay at least 10 feet from honu on land and in the water. Touching, feeding, or crowding them is illegal harassment and can bring fines.
  • Never block a turtle's path to the ocean, and never get between a swimming turtle and the surface.
  • Turtle beaches like Laniakea sit on the North Shore, where winter surf and currents can be dangerous. Watch the water, not just the wildlife, and cross the highway carefully.
