Whale Watching on Oʻahu: Season, Where, and How

Humpback whales migrate to Hawaiian waters each winter to breed and calve, and Oʻahu is a good place to see them. The season runs roughly mid-November through May, with the best odds from January into March. You can spot them from shore or on a boat tour, and there are firm legal rules about keeping your distance.

Quick facts

  • Humpback season on Oʻahu runs roughly mid-November through May, peaking January through March.
  • You can watch from shore at spots like the Makapuʻu Lighthouse Trail and Diamond Head lookouts.
  • Boat tours leave from Kewalo Basin, Waiʻanae, and Koʻolina; the leeward west side often has calmer water.
  • Federal law bans approaching within 100 yards of a humpback by any means, including drones and swimming.
  • Sightings are strongest at peak season but never guaranteed on a given trip.

When to go

North Pacific humpbacks are in Hawaiian waters from about mid-November through May. The peak is January, February, and March, when the most whales are here and breaches, tail slaps, and mother-calf pairs are most common. Early November and late April into May are shoulder weeks with fewer animals around. If seeing whales is a priority, aim for February, then January or March. For how this fits the wider calendar, see the best time to visit Oʻahu.

Watching from shore

You do not need a boat. On a clear winter day you can see spouts, breaches, and tails from elevated coastal spots. The Makapuʻu Lighthouse Trail on the southeast corner is a favorite, with a paved uphill walk to a lookout over deep water. Diamond Head's lookouts, Halona Blowhole, and points along the Waiʻanae coast also work. Bring binoculars, give your eyes time to scan the horizon, and look for the puff of a spout first. The nearby beach at Makapuʻu has its own hazards, so treat the lookout and the water as separate decisions.

Boat tours

Tours leave from several harbors, including Kewalo Basin near Honolulu, Waiʻanae Boat Harbor on the west side, and Koʻolina. The leeward west side often has calmer water and a shorter run out to where whales gather. Boats must follow the same distance rules you do, so a good captain will cut the engine and let whales approach on their own terms rather than chasing them. If you are prone to seasickness, take precautions before boarding, since winter swell can make for a rolling ride.

The distance rules

Humpback whales are federally protected. In Hawaiian waters it is illegal to approach within 100 yards of a humpback by any means, including boat, kayak, paddleboard, drone, or swimming. That applies to you as much as to tour operators. If a whale surfaces close to you, the rule is to hold position or move slowly away, not toward it. Drones count, so do not fly one at whales. These rules exist because harassment disrupts breeding, nursing, and resting.

What you might see

Humpbacks come to Hawaiʻi to mate and give birth, so winter is when you may see calves alongside their mothers, competitive groups of males, and surface behavior like breaching and pectoral-fin slapping. Males also sing long, complex songs underwater. Sightings are never guaranteed on any single trip, but during peak months from a good vantage point your odds are strong. While you are scanning the water, you may also spot other wildlife covered in our guides to sea turtles and Hawaiian monk seals.

Before you go

  • Approaching a humpback within 100 yards is illegal in Hawaiian waters, including by drone, kayak, or swimming. Let whales come to you and keep your distance.
  • Coastal lookouts often sit above rocky, high-surf shorelines. Stay back from wet rocks and cliff edges, and do not confuse a good viewing spot with a safe swimming spot.
  • Winter boat trips can be rough. If you get seasick, prepare before you board.
