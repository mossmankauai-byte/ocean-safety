Maui Beaches: A Guide to Every Shore (with Live Conditions)

Maui's beaches vary enormously by coast: the west and south are the calm, sandy, resort-lined swimming and snorkeling beaches; the north shore is windier and surf-driven; the east is remote rainforest coastline. This guide describes the character of each shore and which beaches suit families, snorkelers, surfers, or people who just want a quiet sit. Every named beach links to its live conditions page, check it before you go, because conditions change daily and we never call any beach safe.

Quick facts

  • West and south shores are calmest for swimming and snorkeling; north shore is surf and wind; east is remote.
  • Big Beach (Mākena), D.T. Fleming, and Hāmoa have strong shore break and currents despite looking inviting.
  • Honolua Bay is prime snorkeling in summer and a serious surf break in winter.
  • Waiʻānapanapa State Park requires an advance reservation to enter.
  • Morning is best for snorkeling before the wind picks up.
  • Each named beach has a live conditions page; check it the day you plan to go.

West Maui beaches (Lāhainā to Kapalua)

The west coast is the postcard resort strip: long stretches of sand, reliable sunsets, and the island's calmest morning snorkeling. It is the best all-around area for families and beginner snorkelers on calm days, though the same north-facing points get big and dangerous in winter surf.

  • Kāʻanapali — long, busy resort beach with a snorkeling point at Black Rock; easy access, good for families.
  • Kapalua Bay — protected crescent, one of the more sheltered swim-and-snorkel spots for beginners.
  • Nāpili Bay — sandy bay good for families; watch for shore break when surf is up.
  • D.T. Fleming — pretty, less crowded, but it has strong shore break and rip currents and a history of rescues; a lifeguarded beach to respect, not underestimate.

South Maui beaches (Kīhei to Mākena)

The south shore is dry and sunny with a long line of accessible beaches, from easy family sand in Kīhei to calmer resort fronts in Wailea to the dramatic and hazardous Mākena.

  • Kamaʻole I — lifeguarded Kīhei family beach with sand and easy entry.
  • Kamaʻole III — popular family beach with a park, lifeguards, and grass; can have shore break.
  • Wailea Beach — calmer resort-front beach, good for an easy swim on gentle days.
  • Big Beach (Mākena) — spectacular wide sand, but a powerful shore break that regularly injures people; this is not a casual swimming beach.

North Shore beaches (Pāʻia and Hoʻokipa)

The north shore is windier, cooler in feel, and defined by surf and windsurfing rather than easy swimming. It is a great place to watch the ocean and see turtles, and a poor place to swim casually, especially in winter.

  • Baldwin Beach — long sandy Pāʻia beach with a lifeguard; a sheltered cove ("Baby Beach") end is calmer, but the main beach has shore break and currents.
  • Baby Beach (Pāʻia) — shallow reef-protected pool popular with families on calm days; still check conditions.
  • Hoʻokipa — world-class windsurf and surf spot where turtles haul out on the sand; better for watching than swimming.

Northwest beaches (Kapalua point and beyond)

Past Kapalua, the coast turns rockier and more exposed. Honolua Bay is the standout: a marine reserve with some of Maui's best summer snorkeling, and one of its best winter surf breaks, which means it flips from calm to serious depending on the season.

  • Honolua Bay — protected snorkeling in summer over rich reef; in winter it is a premier surf spot with strong water movement and not for swimming. Rocky entry, no sand beach.

East Maui and Hāna beaches

The Hāna side is remote, rainy, and dramatic, reached by the long Road to Hāna. The beaches here are about scenery and character, not easy swimming, and services and cell signal are scarce.

  • Waiʻānapanapa — famous black-sand beach in a state park (reservation required to enter); powerful surf and rocky water, more for looking than swimming.
  • Hāna Bay — one of the more protected bays out east, a calmer option on gentle days.
  • Hāmoa — scenic pocket beach with sand; can have strong shore break and currents.

Plan the drive with driving times and the Road to Hāna.

How to choose and check before you go

Match the beach to the people and the day. For young kids and nervous swimmers, favor lifeguarded, protected spots like Kapalua Bay, Kamaʻole I, or Baby Beach on calm days. For snorkeling, go in the morning to leeward beaches. For surf and scenery without swimming, the north shore and Honolua are the show. Wherever you go, pick a lifeguarded beach when you can, and never swim where you're the only one in the water.

Before you go

  • Conditions change daily; a beach that is calm one morning can have dangerous surf and currents the next. Always check the live page and never treat any beach as safe.
  • Big Beach (Mākena) and D.T. Fleming have powerful shore break that regularly causes serious injury; do not dive under or turn your back on the waves.
  • Honolua Bay and the north shore become hazardous surf spots in winter; snorkel Honolua only in calm summer conditions.
  • Never swim at a beach with no one else in the water, and prefer lifeguarded beaches; if there is no lifeguard, be far more conservative.
