Things to Do on Maui: The Honest Local List

Maui's activities are spread across very different regions, from the resort-lined west and south coasts to the rainforest of the east and the volcano in the middle. This is an honest, region-by-region list of what is worth your time, plus what to do when it rains and how to have a good day without spending much. We flag the ocean risks where they matter, because a lot of the best things here happen in or near the water.

Quick facts

  • Activities cluster by region; plan one coast or the volcano per day rather than crossing the island.
  • Morning is best for snorkeling before afternoon wind; leeward coast is calmest.
  • Big Beach (Mākena) and other south/west shores have dangerous shore break; they are not casual swim beaches.
  • Haleakalā sunrise needs a reservation; daytime and sunset do not.
  • Whale season is roughly December to April; turtles are common year-round at spots like Hoʻokipa.
  • The leeward (west/south) coast often stays dry when the east and windward sides are rainy.

West Maui (Lāhainā, Kāʻanapali, Kapalua)

The west side is the classic resort coast: long sandy beaches, sunset views, and the calmest snorkeling in the mornings. Kāʻanapali Beach and its Black Rock point are the busy, easy-access option. Kapalua Bay and Nāpili Bay are more protected and good for beginners on calm days. Honolua Bay, a marine reserve to the north, has some of the best snorkeling on the island in summer, but in winter it becomes a serious surf spot and is not for swimming.

Lāhainā town was heavily affected by the 2023 wildfire; check current status and respect closures and the community before planning around it. Check conditions before snorkeling any of these on the beaches guide, and read when each shore is calm.

South Maui (Kīhei, Wailea, Mākena)

The south shore is dry, sunny, and lined with beaches. The Kamaʻole beaches (I and III) in Kīhei are easy family beaches with lifeguards. Wailea's beaches are calmer and front the resorts. Big Beach (Mākena) is stunning and popular, but it has a powerful shore break that regularly injures people, this is not a casual swimming beach, and its neighbor Little Beach has strong currents.

Morning snorkeling here is good before the afternoon wind. Molokini crater, a snorkel-tour destination, launches from this side. See south-shore live conditions on the beaches guide and read snorkel safety.

The Road to Hāna and East Maui

The Road to Hāna is a full-day drive along a winding, one-lane-bridge coastline through rainforest, past waterfalls, and to black-sand Waiʻānapanapa and the red-tinged Hāna area. The point is the journey. Go early, fill the gas tank, pack food, and don't rush the narrow curves. Many roadside stops have slippery rocks and flash-flood risk in the streams, so stay out of pools when rain is upstream.

Hāmoa and Hāna Bay are the main beaches out this way. Cell service is patchy to nonexistent, so download maps first. See driving times and cell service.

Haleakalā and Upcountry

Haleakalā's summit sits over 10,000 feet up and feels like another planet: cold, thin air, and a crater you can hike into. Sunrise is the famous experience and requires an advance reservation; sunset and daytime visits do not. Bring real warm layers, it can be near freezing while the coast is in the 80s. Upcountry towns like Makawao and Kula offer farms, lavender, and cooler air, a good change of pace from the beach.

Plan sunrise ahead via Haleakalā sunrise reservations.

On the water: whales, turtles, and surf

In winter, whale watching off the leeward coast is reliable and memorable; boats launch from Lāhainā and Māʻalaea. Turtles rest and feed at several spots, including Hoʻokipa on the north shore, where they haul out on the sand; stay back, they are protected by law. Hoʻokipa is also a premier windsurf and surf spot, better for watching than swimming.

See whale watching and seeing sea turtles.

Rainy-day options

Rain usually means the windward and east sides; the leeward coast often stays dry, so a rainy morning is sometimes just a reason to drive to the other side of the island. When it's wet everywhere, good indoor options include the Maui Ocean Center aquarium in Māʻalaea, the Lāhainā and Kīhei shops and galleries, a farm or distillery tour Upcountry, or a spa afternoon. Rain also raises flash-flood risk in streams and waterfalls, so skip the Hāna pools and hiking near rivers on wet days.

Free things to do

A lot of the best of Maui costs nothing. Watch the sunset from any west-side beach, walk the Kāʻanapali beach path, snorkel from shore with your own gear at a calm morning spot, watch surfers and turtles at Hoʻokipa, stroll the Kīhei coastal boardwalk, or drive Upcountry for the views. The beaches themselves are free and public; you only pay for parking in a few spots. Just check conditions first and pick a beach that matches your ability.

Before you go

  • Big Beach (Mākena) shore break regularly injures people; do not treat it as a casual swimming beach, and never dive under or turn your back on the waves. We never call any beach safe.
  • On the Road to Hāna and in East Maui, stay out of stream pools and waterfalls when rain is upstream; flash floods kill.
  • Honolua Bay and Hoʻokipa turn into serious surf spots in winter and are for experienced ocean-goers only then.
  • Keep well back from sea turtles and monk seals; approaching or touching them is illegal.
