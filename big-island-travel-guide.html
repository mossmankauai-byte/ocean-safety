Big Island Travel Guide: Everything for a First Trip

The Big Island holds 63 percent of the Hawaiian archipelago's land and asks more of a first-time visitor than the other islands do. Two airports two hours apart, a 13,803-foot mountain, an erupting volcano, and a coastline that is mostly unguarded. The trip works when you plan it in regions instead of as one loop. This guide covers when to come, how to move around, and what to know before you get in the water.

Quick facts

  • The Big Island covers 4,028 square miles, 63 percent of the Hawaiian archipelago's combined landmass.
  • Kailua-Kona averaged about 19 inches of rain a year from 1981 to 2010; Hilo airport averaged about 127.
  • Hawaiian sea surface temperature runs from 73 or 74 degrees in late February and March to near 80 in late September or early October.
  • Ten Big Island beaches have county lifeguards, on duty 9:30 a.m. to 4:45 p.m. daily.
  • Hawaiʻi Volcanoes National Park is $30 per private vehicle for seven days, card only, and open 24 hours a day.
  • Kīlauea has been erupting episodically from Halemaʻumaʻu since December 23, 2024, with pauses of several days or weeks between episodes.
  • Mauna Kea's summit is 13,803 feet; the access road is paved only to the 9,200-foot Visitor Information Station.
  • Federal law prohibits approaching within 100 yards of humpback whales and within 50 yards of spinner dolphins in Hawaiʻi.

When to go

The National Weather Service in Honolulu describes two Hawaiian seasons: summer between May and October, and winter between October and April. The warmest months are August and September and the coolest are February and March, not December and January, because ocean temperature lags the sun. Sea surface temperature near Hawaiʻi ranges about six degrees, from a low of 73 or 74 between late February and March to a high near 80 in late September or early October.

On this island the season matters less than the side. Kailua-Kona averaged about 19 inches of rain a year over the 1981 to 2010 period and Hilo airport about 127, so a wet winter forecast usually means the windward side while the Kohala coast stays dry. Snow falls on the Mauna Kea summit in winter.

Humpback whales are in Hawaiian waters in winter, and federal law prohibits approaching within 100 yards in Hawaiʻi, including by boat, kayak, drone, when swimming, or by any other vessel. Plan whale trips for the winter months and everything else around what you want the weather to do.

Which airport, and where to base

Kona International (KOA) is the dry side: Kailua-Kona, the Kohala resorts, the coffee belt, and most of the sandy beaches. Hilo International (ITO) is the green side: waterfalls, gardens, the Hāmākua coast, and a 45-minute drive to Hawaiʻi Volcanoes National Park. They are about 1.5 to 2 hours apart by road.

For one base on a short trip, Kona or the Kohala coast is the more forgiving choice, because that is where the sand and the reliable sun are. For a volcano-focused trip, Hilo or the village of Volcano cuts hours of driving. For a week, split it: a few nights each side.

Getting around

You need a rental car. Transit exists but does not do what visitors need, and the Park Service notes rideshare is very difficult and sometimes impossible at the volcano, 30 miles from the nearest population centre with drivers. Saddle Road, renamed the Daniel K. Inouye Highway in 2013, is the fast crossing: 52.7 miles, a maximum elevation of 6,632 feet, rebuilt in sections opened in 2007, 2009 and 2013, and now well paved with a separate lane for slower vehicles on the climbs. The old warnings about it are out of date, and rental companies that once prohibited it now generally allow it.

Everything takes longer than it looks. The Park Service puts Kailua-Kona to Hawaiʻi Volcanoes at 95 miles and 2 to 2.5 hours on Highway 11. Coverage is unreliable on Saddle Road, the summit road, Chain of Craters Road and the remoter coasts, so download offline maps. Full numbers are in the driving times guide.

The ocean, read this before you book anything

This is the part first-timers skip. The state Department of Health puts Hawaiʻi's drowning toll at an average of 83 deaths a year including non-residents, with non-residents the majority of victims at 53 percent over 2013 to 2017, and a non-resident fatal ocean drowning rate about eight times the resident rate. The Big Island's non-resident rate is 51.8 per 100,000, third behind Kauaʻi at 82.5 and Maui at 64.6. More Hawaiʻi visitors drown while snorkelling than during any other activity.

Ten beaches on the whole island have county lifeguards, on duty 9:30 a.m. to 4:45 p.m. daily. On the state's list they are Kahaluʻu, White Sands (Laʻaloa), Hāpuna, Spencer, Punaluʻu, Richardson, Carlsmith, Leleiwi, Onekahakaha and Honoliʻi. Everywhere else, including Kua Bay, Makalawena, Hōnaunau and Papakōlea, you are on your own.

The state's ocean safety guidance is short: know your health limits and fitness level, always swim at a lifeguarded beach, learn to swim and snorkel safely, always swim with a buddy, read and obey posted warning signs, and always be aware of the ocean. Two rules matter everywhere here: never swim at a beach where nobody else is in the water, and never turn your back on the ocean on the black-sand and lava-shelf coasts. The volcano and the mountain

Kīlauea has been erupting episodically from Halemaʻumaʻu since December 23, 2024, with more than fifty episodes by mid-2026, separated by pauses of several days or weeks. The eruption area is closed; viewing is from open points along the caldera rim. Entry to Hawaiʻi Volcanoes National Park is $30 per private vehicle for seven days, card only, and the park is open 24 hours a day. Expect closures and delays from a two-year construction project repairing or removing damaged buildings and infrastructure at the summit, and note that parking lots frequently fill by 10 a.m. Do not plan the trip around lava.

Mauna Kea is the other big commitment. The Visitor Information Station sits at 9,200 feet and is open 9 a.m. to 9 p.m.; the summit is open from 30 minutes before sunrise to 30 minutes after sunset. Above the station you need four-wheel drive in low range on unpaved road, half a tank of fuel, and a vehicle free of mud and invasive species, and around a third of visitors have experienced acute altitude sickness symptoms. The station advises acclimatising at least 30 minutes there, and that people under 13, pregnant, intoxicated, with high blood pressure or a heart or respiratory condition, or who have scuba dived in the prior 24 hours should not go above it.

What to do, by region

Kona is snorkelling, coffee farms and history: Kahaluʻu, Hōnaunau Bay, and Puʻuhonua o Hōnaunau National Historical Park, $20 per private vehicle with gates open daily 8:15 a.m. until sunset. Kealakekua Bay State Historical Park is free and open 7 a.m. to 8 p.m., but all vessels, including kayaks, canoes, stand-up paddleboards and inflatables, need a State Parks permit to transit the bay, launching from Nāpōʻopoʻo wharf is prohibited, and permitted vessels are prohibited from landing at Kaʻawaloa flat below the Captain Cook Monument.

Kohala is the sand: Hāpuna, Kaunaʻoa, Spencer and Waialea, plus Puʻukoholā Heiau and the towns of Hāwī and Kapaʻau. The Hāmākua coast gives you ʻAkaka Falls, which plunges 442 feet into a stream-eroded gorge on a 0.4-mile paved loop, and the Waipiʻo lookout. Hilo is gardens, museums and the farmers market. Kaʻū is black sand, green sand and Ka Lae, the southernmost point of the United States. The things to do list breaks it down region by region.

Practical things

State Parks charges non-residents $5 per person plus $10 per vehicle at Hāpuna, ʻAkaka Falls and Kekaha Kai, credit card only. National park fees are separate: $30 per vehicle at Hawaiʻi Volcanoes, $20 per vehicle at Puʻuhonua o Hōnaunau, or $55 for the Hawaiʻi Tri-Park annual pass covering both plus Haleakalā.

Bring a rain shell and a warm layer regardless of the month, because the east side is wet and the high country is cold. Bring mineral sunscreen: Hawaiʻi law makes it unlawful to sell, offer for sale or distribute sunscreen containing oxybenzone or octinoxate without a prescription, effective January 1, 2021. NOAA Fisheries asks you to stay at least 10 feet from sea turtles and 50 feet from Hawaiian monk seals, on land and in the water, and federal law prohibits approaching within 100 yards of humpback whales and within 50 yards of spinner dolphins. Before you go

  • We never call any beach safe. Non-residents drown in Hawaiʻi at about eight times the resident rate, most often while snorkelling, and only ten beaches on this island have lifeguards. Check live conditions, obey posted signs, and never swim where nobody else is in the water.
  • State Parks warns of dangerous rip currents and pounding shore breaks during periods of high surf at Hāpuna and across Kekaha Kai, and states that waves over three feet are for experts and all others should stay out of the water and away from the shoreline.
  • The Kīlauea eruption area is closed. Volcanic gas, unstable cliffs, ground cracks and falling tephra are the stated hazards, and closures are marked for a reason.
  • Around a third of visitors to Mauna Kea have experienced acute altitude sickness symptoms. Do not drive above the Visitor Information Station within 24 hours of scuba diving, and keep children under 13 below it.
  • Phone coverage is unreliable on Saddle Road, the Mauna Kea summit road, Chain of Craters Road and the remoter coasts. Download offline maps and tell someone your plan.
