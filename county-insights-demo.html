Where people go when a beach turns dangerous

When the app returns a hazard verdict, a share of visitors change their plan — and the next beach they check is the one that just inherited the crowd. That substitution is invisible to every other data source the County has. It is also the single most actionable number here: it tells Ocean Safety and Parks where the displaced people are about to arrive.

Reef protection, from a scanner already in the app

Live in the app

Every beach page in the live app carries a sunscreen scanner: a visitor scans a bottle's barcode, the app reads the active ingredients — not the marketing label — and returns Avoid, Caution, or Reef-safer against Hawaiʻi Act 104, which has banned the sale of oxybenzone and octinoxate sunscreen since January 2021. It shipped to production this month. With visitor opt-in, those scans roll up into two County signals that nothing else on the island produces.

A signal telling you where to look first — never evidence, and never a named store.

Roughly a quarter of scanned bottles carry a filter Act 104 bans from sale. That share, trended by month and by area, is the compliance picture the County has never had.

What these two numbers cannot tell you

Scanned is not sold. Visitors arrive with mainland sunscreen. A scan in Kapaʻa does not mean a Kapaʻa retailer sold it.

Scanned is not applied. A person can scan a bottle, read Avoid, and leave it in the bag — which is the point of the feature.

Density is not reef harm. Warming seas are the dominant stressor; sunscreen filters are an avoidable local one. This ranks where to look, and it is not a verdict on any reef.

No store is ever named. Areas only, thresholded, aggregate. That is a design constraint, not a policy preference.

Sample figures throughout. Real numbers scale with how many visitors reach the guide.

Everything here is visitors as a group — counts and percentages. No individual person, device, or location history is stored, reported, or sold.

Think traffic count, not surveillance. The County receives aggregates, and the aggregates are all that exist.

The data is the Countyʻs. We need help with the QR code.

The full dataset is available to the County — free, aggregate, and permanent. There is no fee and nothing to procure. What OceanSafe needs back is distribution: a QR code at the towers, the beach parks, the visitor centers, and on County materials. The app only produces a signal where visitors actually open it.

The QR is free, co-brandable, and the County controls the wording. It opens the free public guide — no account, no download, no advertisement over a safety verdict. Līhuʻe Airport arrivals is the highest-value placement, and we would take any surface the County or State allows.

Stated plainly: safety is phase one and stays free. Phase two puts that same in-destination audience in front of locally owned Kauaʻi businesses in a separate Town tab, which is what keeps the free safety layer running. The safety verdict is never for sale, and the County's data stays aggregate and free either way.
