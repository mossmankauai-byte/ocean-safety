Maui Travel Guide: Everything for a First Trip

Maui rewards a little planning more than most places. The island is bigger than it looks on a map, the weather swings hard between the sunny leeward coast and the wet windward side, and the ocean is the single thing most first-timers underestimate. This guide walks you through when to come, how to get around, what to do, and the ocean facts that matter before you get in the water.

Quick facts

  • You need a rental car; transit is limited and sights are far apart. Book early.
  • The ocean is the top cause of visitor injury; check conditions and pick beaches for your ability.
  • Whale season runs roughly December to April, peaking January to March.
  • Haleakalā sunrise requires an advance reservation and a roughly 3 a.m. departure.
  • Reef-safe (mineral) sunscreen is required under Maui's Act 104.
  • Spring and fall bring good weather, fewer crowds, and lower prices than winter and summer.

When to go

Maui is a year-round destination, but the seasons feel different. April, May, September, and October tend to bring good weather, thinner crowds, and better prices. Winter (mid-December through March) is peak season: it is the wettest time on the windward side, the busiest for whale watching, and the period when north- and west-facing shores get the largest surf. Summer is dry, hot, and crowded with families.

Whale season runs roughly December through April, with January to March the peak for humpbacks off the leeward coast. If you want the biggest surf on the north shore, come in winter; if you want the calmest water for snorkeling, summer is generally your friend. Getting around

You will want a rental car. Public transit is limited, and the things people come to Maui for, sunrise at Haleakalā, the Road to Hāna, the beaches strung along the west and south coasts, are spread far apart. Book the car early, especially for winter and summer trips, because supply is tight and prices spike.

Driving times are longer than the mileage suggests. Kahului airport to Lāhainā or Kāʻanapali is about 45 minutes to an hour. The Road to Hāna is a full day, not a quick drive. Haleakalā summit for sunrise means leaving your hotel around 3 a.m. and requires a reservation. Plan your days around one region at a time rather than crisscrossing the island.

The ocean (read this first)

This is the part first-timers skip and shouldn't. The ocean is the leading cause of visitor injury and death on Maui, and most incidents happen to people who did not realize conditions had changed or picked the wrong beach for their ability. Waves that look small from the sand can knock you down; currents that are invisible from shore can pull you out.

A few rules that matter everywhere: never turn your back on the ocean, don't swim at a beach with no one else in the water, ask a lifeguard before you get in, and if there is no lifeguard, be far more conservative. Shore break on south and west beaches can slam swimmers into the sand. Winter surf on the north and west shores is for experienced people only.

What to do

The headline experiences are snorkeling, beach days, the Road to Hāna, Haleakalā, and whale watching in season. Snorkeling is best in the morning before the wind picks up, at protected spots on the leeward coast. The Road to Hāna is about the journey, the waterfalls, black-sand beach, and rainforest, not a destination at the end. Haleakalā offers both a sunrise crater experience up top and hiking; the summit is cold and thin-aired even when the coast is warm.

If you want to see turtles, there are reliable spots where they rest and feed; keep your distance, as they are protected. Book whale-watch tours and any Haleakalā sunrise slots well ahead.

Itineraries

Match your plan to your time. A single day, common for cruise-ship passengers docking at the port, means picking one region and one or two activities, not the whole island. Three days is enough for a beach-and-snorkel base plus one big adventure day (Hāna or Haleakalā). Five days lets you do both major drives, several beaches, and still have a rest day.

Practical things to pack and know

Bring reef-safe sunscreen. Maui law (Act 104) restricts sunscreens containing oxybenzone and octinoxate, so buy mineral-based (zinc oxide) sunscreen before or after you arrive. Pack a light rain layer and something warm for Haleakalā, water shoes if you have sensitive feet, and reef-safe options if you plan to snorkel a lot.

Cell service is good in the towns and resort areas and drops out on the Road to Hāna, in Haleakalā, and along remote coastline, so download offline maps and screenshot your reservations. Respect closures and signage; some sites require reservations or have limited parking.

Before you go

  • Do not enter the ocean without checking current conditions; waves and currents change fast and can be dangerous even at popular beaches. We never call any beach safe.
  • Never swim at a beach where no one else is in the water, and ask a lifeguard before getting in.
  • Winter surf on north- and west-facing shores is for experienced ocean-goers only.
  • Cell service disappears on the Road to Hāna, in Haleakalā, and on remote coast; download offline maps and tell someone your plan.
