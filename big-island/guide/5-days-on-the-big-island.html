5 Days on the Big Island: The Full-Island Itinerary

Five days is the first honest length for this island. It is enough to see both sides without living in the car, as long as you split where you sleep. This plan gives three nights to the Kona side and two to the Hilo or Volcano side, crosses the island once on Saddle Road, and reaches Hawaiʻi Volcanoes National Park from 30 miles away instead of 95.

Quick facts

  • The Daniel K. Inouye Highway, or Saddle Road, is 52.7 miles long, tops out at 6,632 feet, and is the shortest route between Hilo and Kailua-Kona.
  • Hawaiʻi Volcanoes National Park is 30 miles and about 45 minutes from Hilo and about 95 miles and 2 to 2.5 hours from Kailua-Kona; entrance is $30 per private vehicle, credit or debit only.
  • Kīlauea has erupted inside Halemaʻumaʻu since December 23, 2024, in intermittent fountaining episodes; the eruption area is closed and viewing is from caldera-rim overlooks.
  • The Maunakea Visitor Information Station sits at 9,200 feet, is open 9 a.m. to 9 p.m. daily, and runs free nightly stargazing programs.
  • Maunakea guidance says do not ascend above the Visitor Information Station if you are pregnant, under 13, have a heart or respiratory condition, or have been SCUBA diving in the prior 24 hours.
  • ʻAkaka Falls State Park is open 8:30 a.m. to 5 p.m., charges non-residents $5 per person plus $10 per vehicle by credit card only, and the falls plunge 442 feet.
  • All vessels must obtain a State Parks permit before transiting Kealakekua Bay, and landing at Kaʻawaloa flat is prohibited.
  • NOAA guidance is to stay at least 10 feet from sea turtles on land and in the water.

Why you split the base

The Daniel K. Inouye Highway, or Saddle Road, is 52.7 miles long and is the shortest driving route between Hilo and Kailua-Kona. Driven once, in the middle of a trip, with your bags in the back, it is a good drive. Driven as a day trip to the volcano and back from Kona, it is hours in the car before you have looked at anything: the Park Service puts Kailua-Kona to the park at about 95 miles and 2 to 2.5 hours each way.

So: three nights on the west side, then move. Sleeping in Hilo or up in Volcano village puts the national park 30 miles and about 45 minutes away and makes an after-dark visit realistic, which is when summit glow shows best.

Days 1–2: Kona and the Kohala coast

Start near the airport and stay close the first afternoon. Kahaluʻu and Laʻaloa, or Magic Sands, both have full-time lifeguards in Kailua-Kona, and both carry hazard flags in our conditions data. North on the Kohala coast, Hāpuna is a state recreation area with lifeguard service that charges non-residents $5 per person plus $10 per vehicle, with Kaunaʻoa and Waialea Bay beside it and no lifeguards at either.

Give one flat morning to Hōnaunau Bay next to Puʻuhonua o Hōnaunau National Historical Park, which is $20 per private vehicle and does not accept cash. Kealakekua Bay just north is a state historical park open 7 a.m. to 8 p.m. with no entrance fee, but all vessels are required to obtain a permit from State Parks before transiting the waters of the bay, and permitted vessels are prohibited from landing at Kaʻawaloa flat or launching from Napoʻopoʻo wharf. Reach the Captain Cook Monument on foot down the steep Kaʻawaloa Trail or with one of the permitted commercial kayak operators.

Day 3: cross the island on Saddle Road

Pack out of Kona in the morning. Highway 190 up toward Waimea, then Route 200, the Daniel K. Inouye Highway, east across the middle of the island. Rental car companies used to prohibit driving it and now allow it, but it still climbs to 6,632 feet between Mauna Kea and Mauna Loa. Fill the tank first.

The Mauna Kea access road turnoff sits near the high point. The Visitor Information Station is at 9,200 feet, is open daily 9 a.m. to 9 p.m., and runs free nightly stargazing programs. That is the stop most people should make.

Down the east side you land in Hilo. If it is raining when you get there, that is not bad luck. Hilo sees measurable precipitation on 273 days a year.

Day 4: Hawaiʻi Volcanoes National Park

Thirty miles and about 45 minutes from Hilo. Entrance is $30 per private vehicle, credit or debit only, and the park is open 24 hours a day, 7 days a week. Go early, because the Park Service warns parking lots can be full by 10 a.m.

Kīlauea has erupted inside Halemaʻumaʻu at the summit since December 23, 2024, in intermittent fountaining episodes rather than continuously, at USGS alert level Orange, Watch, as of late July 2026. The eruption area itself is closed. Viewing is from open rim overlooks including Uēkahuna, Kīlauea Overlook at Kūkamāhuākea, Kūpinaʻi Pali and Keanakākoʻi. Check the USGS Kīlauea update the morning you go, since there may be no episode running.

A full day also covers the Kīlauea Iki Trail down onto the floor of the crater, where the 1959 eruption ponded a lava lake that reached 413 feet deep, and Chain of Craters Road down the slopes of Kīlauea to the sea. Come back after dark if the vents are glowing.

Day 5: Hilo, waterfalls, and the drive back

ʻAkaka Falls State Park is north of Hilo on the Hāmākua coast, open daily 8:30 a.m. to 5 p.m., $5 per person and $10 per vehicle for non-residents, credit card only. The loop footpath is 0.4 miles with stairs, and the falls plunge 442 feet. Rainbow Falls, in Wailuku River State Park at the edge of town, is 80 feet and roadside, open 7 a.m. to 5:30 p.m. with gates locking at 6, and it also charges non-residents $5 per person plus $10 per vehicle.

For water, Richardson Ocean Park and Carlsmith Beach Park east of Hilo both have full-time lifeguards and calm reef-protected pockets. They are rocky rather than sandy, and honu graze in them. NOAA guidance is to stay at least 10 feet, or 3 meters, away on land and in the water.

Then drive back. Budget roughly two hours to Kona, and more than that if you are flying out the same day.

Mauna Kea, honestly

The Visitor Information Station at 9,200 feet is where most visitors should stop. Above it the road is unpaved, rough and quite steep, 4WD only, and the mountain's guidance is to engage 4WD low, downshift and tap the brakes on the descent to avoid brake failure and overheating. Summit access runs from 30 minutes before sunrise to 30 minutes after sunset, and vehicles must be free of invasive species.

The mountain's own public safety guidance is not to go above the Visitor Information Station if you are pregnant, are under 13 years of age, have high blood pressure or a heart or respiratory condition, or are not feeling well. If you want the summit and do not have a proper 4WD, book a tour operator rather than arguing with a rental contract at 13,000 feet.

What still does not fit

Five days does not cover Kaʻū and the far south properly. Punaluʻu black sand beach is on the coastal route if you loop back the long way, but Papakōlea green sand beach is about 3 miles east of Ka Lae, or South Point, is legally accessible only on foot, and the paths there are rugged from erosion and illegal vehicle traffic. It deserves its own morning.

Pololū Valley at the north end is another half day. Access down Waipiʻo Valley Road is limited to Big Island residents, county-permitted tour operators, and people practicing Native Hawaiian traditional and customary rights. Leave these for a return trip rather than shaving hours off the volcano.

Before you go

  • State Parks warns of dangerous rip currents and pounding shorebreak at Hāpuna during periods of high surf, and says waves over 3 feet are for experts with all others staying out. Hawaiʻi's state ocean safety program says shorebreak is an unpredictable and dangerous condition where waves break directly onshore, and that the average wave height when a spinal cord injury occurs is 3 feet.
  • Hōnaunau Bay, Kaunaʻoa, Waialea, and most of the Kaʻū coast have no lifeguard. There is nobody watching, and Two Step has a lava-ledge entry into deep water with no sand to retreat to.
  • The Park Service warns that hazardous volcanic gasses endanger sensitive groups, naming people with heart or respiratory issues, infants, children, and pregnant women. Check air quality before you go and take it seriously.
  • Saddle Road climbs to 6,632 feet, where weather changes fast. Fill up before you leave either side.
  • Rapid altitude gain on Mauna Kea brings shortness of breath, headache, nausea, impaired judgment and loss of balance. Do not drive above the Visitor Information Station within 24 hours of SCUBA diving, and do not take children under 13 up there.
