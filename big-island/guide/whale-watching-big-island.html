Whale Watching on the Big Island: Season, Where, and How

Humpbacks come to Hawaiʻi in winter to breed and calve, then return to Alaska. NOAA's sanctuary gives the viewing window as November through April. Hawaiʻi's tourism authority lists December to May for Hawaiʻi Island. On the Big Island you do not need a boat. The Kohala Coast has enough elevated shoreline to watch from land for free, and Puʻukoholā Heiau above Kawaihae Harbor is the site the state names first.

Quick facts

  • NOAA's sanctuary gives the humpback viewing window as November through April; Go Hawaiʻi lists December to May for Hawaiʻi Island.
  • In the 2025 Sanctuary Ocean Count, 1,353 volunteers counted 4,782 humpback whales from 44 sites across six islands over the event's three days.
  • Federal law prohibits approaching within 100 yards of a humpback whale in Hawaiʻi, including by boat, kayak, drone, or while swimming.
  • A boat within 100 yards of a humpback must stop immediately and leave engines running and out of gear until the whale moves away.
  • NOAA recommends a 15-knot top speed while transiting and a maximum of six knots within 400 yards of a whale.
  • Suspected violations are reported to NOAA Fisheries Enforcement at (800) 853-1964.
  • Congress created the sanctuary in 1992; it lies in water less than 600 feet deep across five separate areas abutting six main islands, with about 150 square miles along Hawaiʻi Island.
  • Federal law prohibits swimming with, approaching, or remaining within 50 yards of spinner dolphins; NOAA recommends at least 10 feet from sea turtles and 50 feet from monk seals, or 150 feet for a mother with a pup.

When they are actually here

The Hawaiian Islands Humpback Whale National Marine Sanctuary says that from November through April, visitors can see whales from shore and at sea. Go Hawaiʻi lists December to May for Hawaiʻi Island. The two windows overlap from December through April, which is the stretch to plan a trip around.

The sanctuary's Ocean Count gives a sense of scale. In 2025, 1,353 volunteers counted whales from 44 different sites across Hawaiʻi Island, Maui, Molokaʻi, Lānaʻi, Oʻahu, and Kauaʻi. A total of 4,782 humpbacks were counted over the event's three days.

Watching from shore

Shore watching costs nothing and works well here. You want height and an open western horizon. Puʻukoholā Heiau National Historic Site above Kawaihae Harbor is the site Go Hawaiʻi calls out first, and the Kohala beaches give you long open water. Bring binoculars. Look for the puff of a spout first, then keep your eyes on that patch of water for several minutes rather than scanning.

Reasonable places to stand and look:

  • Puʻukoholā Heiau National Historic Site, on the bluff above Kawaihae.
  • Hāpuna Beach, for a wide open western view.
  • Kaunaʻoa Beach, at the north end of the same stretch.
  • Hilo Bay on the east side, which Go Hawaiʻi also names.

Wind generally builds through the day, so morning is usually the easiest time to read the water surface.

Taking a boat

Two harbors serve most trips. Honokōhau Harbor sits about three miles north of Kailua-Kona, at the south end of Kaloko-Honokōhau National Historical Park, and is the closest launch to the Kona hotels. Kawaihae Harbor is the shorter drive from the Kohala Coast resorts. Both put you in leeward water on the west side of the island, which is the practical argument for doing this on the Kona side at all.

Because wind builds through the day, morning departures are generally smoother than afternoon ones. Small rafts sit lower to the water and take more of a beating; larger catamarans are steadier if anyone in the group is prone to seasickness. Take motion-sickness medication before you board.

The 100-yard rule

Federal law prohibits approaching within 100 yards of a humpback whale in Hawaiʻi. That applies by boat, by kayak, by drone, when swimming, or by any other vessel or means. It is not a courtesy guideline, and it does not stop applying because the whale came to you.

If you are running a boat and end up within 100 yards, NOAA's instruction is to stop immediately, leave the engines running and out of gear, and wait until the whale moves away. NOAA also recommends a top speed of 15 knots or safe planing speed while transiting, and a maximum of six knots within 400 yards of a whale until you are more than 400 yards off. Report suspected violations to NOAA Fisheries Enforcement at (800) 853-1964.

What the sanctuary covers here

Congress created the sanctuary in 1992 to protect humpbacks and their habitat. It lies in the shallow waters surrounding the main Hawaiian Islands, less than 600 feet deep, and is made up of five separate areas abutting six of the main islands. The Hawaiʻi Island piece is the sanctuary's southeastern-most section, about 150 square miles.

Worth being clear about: the 100-yard approach rule is federal law throughout Hawaiʻi, not something that switches on only when you cross a sanctuary line. Inside the boundary off Kawaihae or well outside it, the distance is the same.

The other animals you will see

Spinner dolphins are common in the same leeward bays and carry their own federal limit: it is illegal to swim with, approach, or remain within 50 yards of a spinner dolphin in Hawaiʻi. They rest in shallow water during the day, and a boat or swimmer pushing in on a resting pod is doing real harm even when the dolphins look playful.

Green sea turtles are common along this coast. NOAA gives at least 10 feet, on land and in the water, as the recommended viewing distance for them, at least 50 feet for Hawaiian monk seals, and at least 150 feet for a mother seal with a pup. Those are recommended distances; the 100-yard humpback limit and the 50-yard spinner dolphin limit are federal law. Go Hawaiʻi also notes other whale species are in these waters year round, so a sighting outside the humpback window is not impossible. Check live conditions before any shoreline outing, and see the manta ray night snorkel guide for the island's other well-known wildlife trip.

Before you go

  • Whale season is also winter surf season. Shore watching pulls people onto rocks, bluffs, and wave-exposed points for a better angle. Check live conditions, keep back from the wet zone on any lava shelf, and never turn your back on the water.
  • Stay 100 yards from humpbacks by any means, including swimming and drones. If a whale surfaces near you, stop and let it move off rather than closing the gap.
  • Do not approach or swim with spinner dolphins. Federal law sets a 50-yard limit, and the shallow bays they use during the day are where they rest.
  • Open-channel boat trips get rougher as the wind builds through the day. Take motion-sickness medication before boarding, and pick a morning departure if anyone in the group is prone to it.
  • Use binoculars from a fixed vantage point rather than walking out onto wet rock for a closer angle. Surf breaking over a low shelf is the hazard on this coast, not the whales.
