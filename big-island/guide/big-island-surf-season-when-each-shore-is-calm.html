Big Island Surf Season: When Each Shore Is Calm (and When It Is Not)

The Big Island has four coasts and they are never all calm at once. The state's ocean safety guidance splits the year into a summer season from May through October and a winter season from November through April, and each shore direction behaves differently in each. Picking the coast that is out of season for swell matters more than picking the beach. Here is how each shore reads through the year.

Quick facts

  • The state's ocean safety guidance runs a summer season from May through October and a winter season from November through April.
  • North shores, including Pololū, get consistent huge and dangerous swells in the November through April winter season.
  • West shores including Hāpuna, Kaunaʻoa, Kua Bay and Makalawena are listed as taking wrapped north swell in both seasons, so the Kohala and Kona coast has no fully calm season.
  • South shores including Punaluʻu, Honuʻapo, Ka Lae and Papakōlea get large swells frequently from May through October and are flat-to-small in winter.
  • The state uses the same wording for east shores in both seasons: strong trade winds can create large, rough waves.
  • The state parks warning for Hāpuna is that waves over 3 feet high are for experts and all others should stay out of the water.
  • Hawaiʻi Island has exactly ten lifeguarded beaches on the state's list, all staffed 9:30 a.m. to 4:45 p.m., seven days a week.
  • Kona skies are clearest between 8 and 10 a.m. and reach about 80 percent cloud cover by 2 p.m.

Winter: the north and west shores take the hits

For the winter season, November through April, the state describes north shores as getting consistent huge and dangerous swells, and says inexperienced surfers and swimmers of any skill level should avoid entering the water at those beaches during high-surf conditions. On this island the north-facing shore is Pololū Valley. West shores get north swell wrapping around to create high surf, and the state lists that in both seasons, so the Kohala and Kona strip is never fully out of season: Hāpuna, Kaunaʻoa, Waialea, Mauʻumae, Spencer, Kīholo, Kua Bay, Makalawena and Mahaiʻula all face roughly west.

Hāpuna is the one that catches visitors out. It is a wide white-sand beach with a lifeguard, and the state parks warning for it is direct: dangerous rip currents and pounding shore breaks during periods of high surf, and waves over 3 feet high are for experts, with all others to stay out of the water. Check Hāpuna conditions and Kua Bay conditions before you commit to a beach day up there.

Summer: the Kaʻū south coast turns on

In the summer season, May through October, the state says large swells can arrive frequently on south shores; in winter it lists those same shores as flat-to-small. On the Big Island that means the Kaʻū coast: Punaluʻu, Whittington and Honuʻapo, Kaʻalualu, Papakōlea and Ka Lae. The more southwest-facing Kona spots pick up the same swell, and Kahaluʻu faces south-southwest.

This coast has almost no lifeguard coverage. Punaluʻu is the only beach in Kaʻū on the state's lifeguarded list. Papakōlea, Ka Lae, Kaʻalualu and Whittington are not on it, and they have no shade and long walks or rough tracks between you and help.

The Hilo and Puna side is a trade-wind coast year round

The east side does not have an off-season. The state's ocean safety guidance uses identical wording for east shores in both the summer and the winter season: strong trade winds can create large, rough waves. That is an honest way of saying the exposure never really goes away, and it lines up with trade winds prevailing 80 to 95 percent of the time in summer and 50 to 80 percent in winter.

Honoliʻi, an east-northeast facing rivermouth break north of Hilo, is one of the island's best-known surf spots and is at its best in winter. Pohoiki and Kehena in Puna face south-southeast and pick up both trade swell and summer south swell. The Hilo bayfront parks, Richardson, Carlsmith, Leleiwi and Onekahakaha, sit behind reef and rock and are the more protected end of that coast, but protected is not calm.

Where it is protected by geography rather than by season

A few spots owe their shelter to shape rather than the calendar. Kamakahonu, the small beach behind Kailua Pier in Kailua-Kona, sits inside the pier and is one of the more sheltered pockets of water on the west side. Onekahakaha in Hilo has a rock-walled shallow pond and is one of the ten lifeguarded beaches on the island. Hōnaunau Bay, the Two Step entry, has no sand at all: you get in and out over a bare lava ledge, there is no lifeguard, and a real swell makes the exit difficult.

Kahaluʻu deserves a specific warning. A breakwater reef takes much of the wave energy, and nearly half a million people visit each year, which makes it one of the most popular snorkel spots on the island and one with a full-time lifeguard. The Kahaluʻu Bay Education Center still tells people to enter and exit through the channel in front of the lifeguard tower and warns that large waves, strong currents, sharp rocks and sea urchins are common and that conditions in the bay can change rapidly. It also asks visitors to wait for the lifeguards before entering the water.

Mornings beat afternoons on the Kona side

The leeward coast runs on a land and sea breeze cycle rather than steady trades. Kona skies are clearest from 8 to 10 a.m. and reach roughly 80 percent cloud cover by 2 p.m., and the leeward side's rain is an afternoon pattern rather than an all-day one.

That is the practical reason the west-side boats leave early. If you want the calmest, clearest part of the Kona day, be in the water in the morning and treat the afternoon as land time.

How to read a day here

Check the forecast for the shore you are going to, not for the island. A north swell means nothing to Punaluʻu and a great deal to Hāpuna. Then watch the actual water for a full ten minutes before you get in, and count the waves in a set so you see the big ones rather than the average ones.

Hawaiʻi Island has ten lifeguarded beaches, all staffed 9:30 a.m. to 4:45 p.m., Monday through Sunday: Hāpuna, Spencer, Kahaluʻu and White Sands (Laʻaloa) on the west side, and Punaluʻu, Honoliʻi, Richardson, Carlsmith, Leleiwi and Onekahakaha on the east and south. Everything else, including Kua Bay, Makalawena, Papakōlea, Pololū and Two Step, is not on that list. If you are unsure, pick a coast that is out of season for swell and a beach where somebody is on the tower. Start with today's conditions.

Before you go

  • Never dive into shorebreak head first. The state's guidance says this can drive you into the ocean floor and cause serious injury to the head, neck and spinal cord, that the average wave height when spinal cord injuries occur is just 3 feet, and that you should duck under oncoming waves instead.
  • At Hāpuna, state parks warns of dangerous rip currents and pounding shore breaks during high surf, and says waves over 3 feet high are for experts and everyone else should stay out of the water.
  • At Kahaluʻu, enter and exit only through the channel in front of the lifeguard tower. Large waves, strong currents, sharp rocks and sea urchins are common, conditions in the bay change rapidly, and the guidance is to wait for the lifeguards before entering the water.
  • Ka Lae, Papakōlea, Kaʻalualu and Whittington on the Kaʻū coast are not on the state's lifeguarded list. There is no shade and a long way to any help. Do not enter the water there because other people are.
  • Outside 9:30 a.m. to 4:45 p.m., and at every beach not on the state lifeguard list, nobody is watching the water. A shore that was flat yesterday can have dangerous surf today when a new swell fills in, so check the forecast for that specific shore every time.
