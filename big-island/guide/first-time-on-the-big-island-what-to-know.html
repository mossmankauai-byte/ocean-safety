First Time on the Big Island: What to Know Before You Land

The Big Island is not a scaled-up Maui. It is 4,028 square miles with two airports on opposite coasts, a rainforest on one side and near-desert on the other, an erupting volcano in the middle, and ten lifeguarded beaches for the entire island. Most first-trip problems here are planning problems, not bad luck. Here is what to sort out before the plane doors open.

Quick facts

  • KOA and ITO are on opposite coasts, about 1.5 to 2 hours apart over Saddle Road.
  • Hilo airport averaged about 127 inches of rain a year from 1981 to 2010; Kailua-Kona averaged about 19.
  • Kīlauea has been erupting episodically from Halemaʻumaʻu since December 23, 2024, with eruptions as short as a few hours and pauses of several days or weeks.
  • Hawaiʻi Volcanoes National Park charges $30 per private vehicle, does not accept cash, and is open 24 hours a day, seven days a week.
  • Only ten Big Island beaches have county lifeguards, on duty 9:30 a.m. to 4:45 p.m. daily.
  • Non-residents were 53 percent of Hawaiʻi drowning victims in 2013 to 2017, with a fatal ocean drowning rate about eight times the resident rate.
  • The Mauna Kea summit requires four-wheel drive in low range above the 9,200-foot Visitor Information Station, and around a third of visitors have experienced acute altitude sickness symptoms.
  • Waipiʻo Valley Road access is limited to residents, county-permitted operators and Native Hawaiian traditional and customary practice; the lookout at the top is open to all.

Pick the right airport, or use both

Kona International (KOA) serves the dry west side, the Kohala resorts and the coffee belt. Hilo International (ITO) serves the wet east side, the Hāmākua coast and Hawaiʻi Volcanoes National Park. They are about 1.5 to 2 hours apart over Saddle Road. Booking the wrong one can cost you four hours of driving on a short trip.

If your plan crosses the island anyway, flying into one and out of the other is usually the better trip. Rental companies charge a fee for a one-way drop-off between the two, so confirm it before you commit. The numbers are in the driving times guide.

You need a rental car

The county Hele-On bus does reach Hawaiʻi Volcanoes National Park, dropping off and picking up at the intersection of Volcano House and the second entrance to Kīlauea Visitor Center, but the Park Service notes its schedules are subject to change and delays and that there is no public transport or shuttle within the park. It also states that the park sits 30 miles from the closest population centre with rideshare drivers, which makes rideshare apps very difficult and sometimes impossible to use there. Book the car when you book the flight.

Read the rental agreement's road exclusions. Saddle Road is usually fine now. Waipiʻo Valley Road is closed to visitors regardless, and the Mauna Kea summit road above the Visitor Information Station is unpaved and requires four-wheel drive in low range, so a tow off it is on you.

Two islands' worth of weather

Hilo International Airport averaged about 127 inches of rain a year between 1981 and 2010. Kailua-Kona averaged about 19 over the same period, sheltered on the leeward side of Hualālai. A single forecast for the Big Island is nearly meaningless, because it is describing two different climates at once, plus a 13,803-foot mountain that gets snow.

Practically: pack a rain shell for the east side and a warm layer for the high country even in July, and do not cancel a Hilo day because the app says rain. Check the conditions where you actually are on the live conditions hub.

The volcano is erupting, on its own schedule

Kīlauea has been erupting episodically from Halemaʻumaʻu at the summit since December 23, 2024, and was still going through mid-2026, past its fiftieth episode. The Park Service puts the pattern plainly: eruptions can be as short as a few hours, and pauses between them last several days or weeks. Do not build a trip around seeing lava. Build it around seeing the park, and treat an active episode as a bonus.

Entry is $30 per private vehicle, valid seven days. The park does not accept cash, only credit or debit. The Hawaiʻi Tri-Park annual pass is $55 and also covers Haleakalā and Puʻuhonua o Hōnaunau. The park is open 24 hours a day, seven days a week, including holidays, which matters because the glow reads best after dark. The eruption area itself is closed; you view from open points along the caldera rim. The Park Service also warns of closures and delays from a two-year construction project repairing or removing damaged buildings and infrastructure at the summit, and that parking lots frequently fill by 10 a.m.

The ocean here is less forgiving than it looks

The state Department of Health counts an average of nearly 40 resident drowning deaths a year, rising to 83 when non-residents are included. Non-residents were the majority of victims at 53 percent over the 2013 to 2017 period, and the fatal ocean drowning rate for non-residents in Hawaiʻi is about eight times higher than for residents. The Big Island's non-resident fatal ocean drowning rate is 51.8 per 100,000, third among the main islands behind Kauaʻi at 82.5 and Maui at 64.6. The department also states that more Hawaiʻi visitors drown while snorkelling than during any other activity.

Ten beaches on this island have county lifeguards, on duty 9:30 a.m. to 4:45 p.m. daily. Everywhere else you are on your own, including some of the most photographed spots. The state's own park pages carry blunt warnings: Hāpuna and Kekaha Kai both warn of dangerous rip currents and pounding shore breaks during periods of high surf, and Kekaha Kai has no lifeguard services at all. Read the beaches guide before you pick a spot.

Vog, and who it affects

Vog forms when sulfur dioxide and other gases and particles from an erupting volcano react with oxygen and moisture in the presence of sunlight. Trade winds push it south-west, which is why the Kona coast on the west side gets the most frequent exposure and the sunny side often looks hazy. Prolonged southerly Kona winds can send it the other way, reaching the east side of the island and islands across the state.

Reported symptoms include headaches, watery eyes, sore throat, breathing difficulties including induced asthma attacks, flu-like symptoms and lethargy, and the effects are especially pronounced in children and in people with respiratory conditions. If you use an inhaler, bring it and keep it with you rather than in the room. The Park Service advises checking air quality before a visit.

Three places that are not casual stops

Mauna Kea tops out at 13,803 feet. The Visitor Information Station sits at 9,200 feet and is open 9 a.m. to 9 p.m.; the summit is open from 30 minutes before sunrise to 30 minutes after sunset. Above the station you need four-wheel drive in low range, at least half a tank of fuel, and a vehicle free of invasive species and mud. The station's own guidance is to acclimatise at least 30 minutes there, and that people under 13, pregnant, intoxicated, with high blood pressure or a heart or respiratory condition, or who have scuba dived in the prior 24 hours should not go above it. Around a third of visitors have experienced symptoms of acute altitude sickness.

Waipiʻo Valley Road is currently open to the valley floor only for island residents, county-permitted tour operators, and people practising Native Hawaiian traditional or customary rights. The lookout is open to everyone. Papakōlea Green Sand Beach, about three miles east of Ka Lae, is legally accessible only on foot over rugged paths, with no shade, no drinking water and no facilities.

Before you go

  • No Big Island beach is safe by default, and most have no lifeguard. Non-residents drown here at about eight times the resident rate, most often while snorkelling or swimming. Check live conditions, obey posted warning signs, and pick a lifeguarded beach when you can.
  • State Parks warns of dangerous rip currents and pounding shore breaks during periods of high surf at both Hāpuna and Kekaha Kai, and advises that waves over three feet are for experts and all others should stay out of the water and away from the shoreline.
  • Do not plan a trip around seeing lava. Kīlauea's eruption is episodic, pauses can last weeks, and the eruption area itself is closed to the public.
  • Vog can induce asthma attacks and breathing difficulties, and the effects are especially pronounced in children and people with respiratory conditions. Carry your inhaler and check air quality before spending a day near the volcano.
  • Altitude on Mauna Kea is a medical risk, not a nuisance. The Visitor Information Station advises that people under 13, pregnant, intoxicated, with high blood pressure or a heart or respiratory condition, or who have scuba dived in the prior 24 hours should not go above it.
