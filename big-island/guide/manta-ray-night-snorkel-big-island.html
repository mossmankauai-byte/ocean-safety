The Kona Manta Ray Night Snorkel: How It Works and How to Do It Safely

Kona's manta ray night snorkel puts you in the ocean after dark to watch reef mantas feed. Boats leave around sunset from Keauhou Bay and from Honokōhau Harbor north of Kailua-Kona, stop over a lit patch of sand, and you float face-down holding a lighted board while the mantas eat the plankton the lights gather. It is open water, at night, off a boat, and it is not risk-free.

Quick facts

  • Hawaiʻi has two designated manta ray viewing zones in West Hawaiʻi: Makako Bay (Garden Eel Cove) off the Kona airport and Kaukalaelae Point at Keauhou Bay, both inside the West Hawaiʻi Ocean Recreation Management Area.
  • There is no adopted state rule specific to manta viewing; the Board of Land and Natural Resources approved starting rulemaking on October 27, 2022.
  • The proposed rules would limit commercial viewing permits to 24 per zone, set viewing hours of 4:00 p.m. to 4:00 a.m., run two-hour vessel shifts, cap boats at 60 passengers for hire per 24 hours, require an 8-to-1 guide-to-customer ratio, and charge $300 monthly.
  • Hawaiʻi Revised Statutes §188-39.5 makes it illegal to knowingly capture, take, possess, abuse, or entangle a ray, or to kill one, in state marine waters.
  • Criminal fines under §188-39.5 run $500 for a first offense, $2,000 for a second, and $10,000 for a third or later offense, with administrative fines up to $10,000 per ray.
  • Hawaii Ocean Watch suspended its Manta Ray Green List in 2023, citing 15 to 30 boats circulating in the dark while 200 to 400 people were in the water.
  • The Green List standards were: no touching, no free diving, no hull lighting, no propellers, no anchoring on reef, and no fragmentation of the group.
  • A 62-year-old visitor from Portland, Oregon died after a guided manta snorkel out of Keauhou Pier on April 30, 2025; police opened a coroner's inquest and suspect no foul play.

What actually happens

The lights are the whole mechanism. Downward-facing lights gather plankton, the mantas come to eat the plankton, and you watch from a few feet away. Snorkelers hang on a floating light board at the surface with their legs up and still. Scuba divers stay on the sand at the bottom. The water column in between is left empty so the animals have room to loop and barrel-roll through it.

Most of the trip is briefing, transit, and gear, and the time actually in the water is a much shorter window than the trip length suggests. Floating still at the surface after dark gets cold, so ask whether the boat provides wetsuits.

The two sites

The state has designated two manta ray viewing zones in West Hawaiʻi, both inside the West Hawaiʻi Ocean Recreation Management Area: Makako Bay, known in daylight as Garden Eel Cove, offshore of the Kona airport, and Kaukalaelae Point at Keauhou Bay south of Kailua-Kona.

Which one you go to matters less than how long the boat ride is. If anyone in your group gets seasick, that difference is the whole evening. Check live conditions the afternoon of your trip, because Kona's leeward water is usually calmer than the windward side but not always calm.

The no-touch rule

Do not touch a manta. Their skin carries a protective mucus layer that a hand wipes off. Do not chase one, do not swim under one, and do not free-dive down to get a photo from below. Manta rays have no stinging barb, which is exactly why people forget themselves and reach out. Keep your hands to your chest.

This is also the law. Hawaiʻi Revised Statutes §188-39.5 makes it illegal to knowingly capture, take, possess, abuse, or entangle a ray, or to kill one, in state marine waters. Criminal fines run $500 for a first offense, $2,000 for a second, and $10,000 for a third or later offense, with administrative fines of up to $10,000 per ray on top of that.

The Green List, and what it means that it is gone

Hawaii Ocean Watch ran a Manta Ray Green List that certified operators against a written standard of conduct. It suspended the list in 2023, saying it had become virtually impossible for companies operating at the Keauhou or airport viewing sites to comply, with 15 to 30 boats dwelling or circulating in the dark while 200 to 400 people were in the water.

The standards themselves are still a usable yardstick, whether or not anyone is certifying against them:

  • No touching a manta.
  • No free diving down to the animals.
  • No lights mounted on the boat hull.
  • No propellers.
  • No anchoring on reef.
  • No fragmenting the group.

There are still no state rules

This is the part most booking pages leave out. There is no adopted state rule specific to manta viewing. In October 2022 the Board of Land and Natural Resources approved starting rulemaking for the two West Hawaiʻi zones. The proposal would limit commercial viewing permits to 24 in each zone, define manta ray viewing hours as 4:00 p.m. to 4:00 a.m. the next day, run vessels in two-hour shifts in each zone, allow no more than 60 passengers for hire per boat within any 24-hour period, require a guide-to-customer ratio of 8-to-1, and charge a fixed monthly fee of $300 on top of commercial use permit fees.

None of that is in force. No manta ray viewing rules appear among the adopted ocean recreation rules the Division of Boating and Ocean Recreation lists, which carry amendments dated through April 2025. Until rules are adopted, the number of boats on any given night, and the conduct on them, depends on the company you picked.

The honest risk picture

On April 30, 2025, a 62-year-old visitor from Portland, Oregon died after a guided manta ray snorkel out of Keauhou Pier. Police say she was in the water about five minutes, returned to the boat saying her arms were sore and that she was not feeling well, then became unresponsive shortly afterward. Crew members administered CPR and she was pronounced dead at Kona Community Hospital. Police opened a coroner's inquest to determine the cause of death and said no foul play was suspected.

Stack the conditions honestly. It is dark. You cannot stand up. You are in open water off a boat, sometimes among many boats. You are floating still and getting cold. If you are not a comfortable swimmer, if you have a heart or lung condition, or if you have never worn a snorkel before, this is the wrong trip for your first attempt. Try a mask in daylight somewhere shallow first, like Kahaluʻu Beach Park, and see how breathing through it feels.

How to book it well

Sightings are not guaranteed, and operators handle a no-show night differently, so ask what their policy actually is before you pay. Ask how many people are on the board, whether wetsuits are included, whether they run a separate float for weak swimmers, and how they handle someone who wants out early.

Book early in your trip so a weather cancellation leaves you a second shot. Go on a night you are rested, not the night you landed. If the crew cancels for conditions, that is the system working.

Before you go

  • This is open water at night, off a boat, with no bottom under you. Calm-looking water is not safe water. Check live conditions, and if breathing through the snorkel feels like effort at any point, get back on the boat immediately.
  • Never touch, chase, or dive down on a manta. Handling a ray is a crime under Hawaiʻi law, with criminal fines up to $10,000 and administrative fines up to $10,000 per animal.
  • Do not make this your first time in a mask. Test your gear in shallow daylight water where you can stand before committing to a night trip in open water.
  • No state rules govern these tours yet, so crowding and conduct vary entirely by company. Ask how many boats and people are typically at the site on your night, and book the operator that answers straight.
  • Cold and seasickness end more of these trips than anything else. Ask whether wetsuits are included, and take motion-sickness medication before boarding, not after.
