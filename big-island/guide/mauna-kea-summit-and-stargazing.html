Mauna Kea: Summit Access, Stargazing, and What to Bring

Mauna Kea's summit is 13,803 feet, and the paved road ends well below it at the Maunakea Visitor Information Station, 9,200 feet up. Above the station the road is unpaved and four-wheel-drive only, and the mountain's own guidance tells drivers to check whether their rental company allows it. The summit area closes 30 minutes after sunset, so evening stargazing happens lower down. Here is how access, altitude, and weather work on this mountain.

Quick facts

  • Mauna Kea's summit is 13,803 feet; the Maunakea Visitor Information Station sits at 9,200 feet.
  • The station is open 9 a.m. to 9 p.m., 365 days a year, with no registration, entrance, or parking fee.
  • Two-wheel-drive vehicles are not permitted above the station, and the eight-mile summit road rises nearly 5,000 feet.
  • The summit area is closed to visitors from 30 minutes after sunset to 30 minutes before sunrise; stargazers must descend to Park 2 or below.
  • Visitors are asked to acclimate at the station for at least 30 minutes before driving higher.
  • Do not go above the station if you are under 13, pregnant, intoxicated, have scuba dived in the prior 24 hours, or have high blood pressure or a heart or respiratory condition.
  • The nightly public telescope program was halted in 2019; a free once-a-month telescope event now runs with limited capacity and a required reservation.
  • The nearest gas stations are in Hilo, Waikoloa Village, and Waimea, each about 35 miles away.

Two different trips: the station and the summit

The Maunakea Visitor Information Station sits at 9,200 feet on the Maunakea Access Road, at Milepost 28 off the Daniel K. Inouye Highway (Saddle Road). It is open 9 a.m. to 9 p.m., 365 days a year, and there is no registration, entrance, or parking fee. Restrooms are outside at the back of the building, and bathroom hours are subject to change based on daily water usage. Food is limited to small snacks for purchase when available, and water is the only drink sold.

The pavement ends about 300 yards past the station. Above that, the eight-mile summit road rises nearly 5,000 feet. Going higher is a separate decision with separate requirements. Driving to the station, watching the sun set from Puʻukalepeamoa, and staying for the stars is a full evening on its own.

Getting above the Visitor Information Station

Two-wheel-drive vehicles are not permitted above the station. A dependable, fully operable 4WD or AWD vehicle is required. Past the end of the pavement, the next four and a half miles are a steep graded-gravel road, and unpaved sections are only wide enough for single-lane traffic. Listed road hazards include atmospheric and solar glare, blind curves, rock debris, and poor traction. On the descent the instruction is to engage 4WD low range, downshift, and tap the brakes, specifically to avoid brake overheating and failure.

Read your rental agreement. The Maunakea visitor site tells drivers to check with their rental car company to see whether they are allowed to travel on Maunakea. Visibly dirty or muddy vehicles will be turned away, and vehicles and gear are supposed to be clean to avoid spreading invasive species.

You are responsible for your own safety on the mountain, and emergency assistance may be hours away. Park only in clearly marked areas or as directed by a Ranger. Minors must be accompanied by an adult at all times. There are no shuttles to the summit; permitted commercial tour services all start from Kona or Hilo and must be prearranged in town.

Altitude is the real hazard

Acclimate at the station for at least 30 minutes before going higher. At 14,000 feet there is 40 percent less oxygen available than at sea level. The safety guidance lists the signs of altitude sickness as thirst, shortness of breath, headache, nausea, impaired judgment, fatigue or drowsiness, and loss of balance and muscle coordination. Descend immediately and seek medical attention if any of them appear. Altitude sickness can lead to high altitude pulmonary edema and high altitude cerebral edema, which are life-threatening.

Do not travel above the station if you are under 13, are pregnant, are intoxicated, have been scuba diving in the prior 24 hours, or have high blood pressure or a heart or respiratory condition. Anyone in poor health should consult a physician before planning a visit. The scuba rule rules out the combination of a morning dive on the Kona coast and an afternoon drive up the mountain on the same day.

Where the stargazing actually happens

The summit area is closed to visitors from 30 minutes after sunset to 30 minutes before sunrise. There is no public stargazing at the top. Visitors may remain on Maunakea after dark to stargaze, but must descend to Park 2 or below to do it.

There are no formal nighttime programs at the station. After twilight ends, staff step outside and give a laser-guided presentation. Topics vary with staff and time of year and can cover Greek constellations, Hawaiian starlines, wayfinding, notable objects and discoveries in space, and astrophotography. This is weather and staff dependent.

There are no public-use telescopes at the station in the evening. The nightly telescope program was halted in 2019 because of safety concerns and overcrowding that was causing environmental damage. A free once-a-month telescope event now runs instead, with limited capacity and a required reservation.

The lower site is not a worse view. The visitor site states that the lack of oxygen on the summit means your vision is not as acute there, and that you can actually see more stars from the station than from the summit.

Weather and when to turn around

The mountain's safety guidance states that winter conditions may occur at any time, causing freezing temperatures, 100-plus mph winds, and zero-visibility whiteouts. Ice on the ground and falling ice from observatories and other structures are both listed hazards. Snow play is at your own risk, and snowmobiles and devices lacking directional or braking mechanisms, such as inner tubes and boogie boards, are prohibited.

Check road and weather status before you leave the coast. The Maunakea weather hotline is an automated line at (808) 935-6268, and current road and weather information is posted on the visitor site. Obey all road and area closures. If you become stuck in a severe winter storm, stay in your vehicle and turn the engine off to avoid carbon monoxide poisoning.

What is not up there

There are no public gas stations in the area. The stations in Hilo, Waikoloa Village, and Waimea are each about 35 miles away, and the recommendation is at least half a tank before proceeding to the summit. Maunakea has no public accommodations, and most observatory buildings are not open to the public. Camping and overnight sleeping are prohibited, as are fires, including those contained in grills or portable stoves. Drones are prohibited. Pets are not allowed except service animals and hunting dogs.

Cell-phone service is spotty and absent in places. Above the Visitor Information Station, radio, cellphone, Bluetooth, and Wi-Fi use is restricted to emergencies because of interference with the radio telescopes. A public emergency telephone is located at the University of Hawaiʻi 2.2 meter Telescope. Download maps and directions before you drive up. The Maunakea Rangers' line for minor emergencies on the mountain is (808) 960-2952, the station is (808) 934-4550, and 911 is for major emergencies.

Bring cold-weather clothing. Hypothermia and intense solar radiation are both listed hazards, with guidance to use adequate cold-weather clothing, limit your exposure to the cold, and protect yourself with appropriate clothing, sunscreen, and sunglasses. The summit air is extremely dry, and the suggested intake is about 16.9 fluid ounces (500 ml) per person per hour. The packing guide covers the rest.

Before you go

  • Do not travel above the Visitor Information Station if you have been scuba diving in the prior 24 hours. A morning dive on the Kona coast rules out the summit that day.
  • Descend in 4WD low range, downshifting and tapping the brakes. The official instruction exists to avoid brake overheating and failure on four and a half miles of steep graded gravel.
  • Impaired judgment and loss of balance are listed signs of altitude sickness. If anyone in the vehicle has a headache, nausea, or trouble balancing, descend immediately and seek medical attention.
  • Winter conditions may occur at any time of year, causing freezing temperatures, 100-plus mph winds, and zero-visibility whiteouts. Call the weather hotline at (808) 935-6268 before leaving the coast and obey all road and area closures.
  • If you pair a summit day with a beach day, treat the ocean on its own terms. Big Island shore conditions change through the day and many beaches have no lifeguard. Check live conditions before you swim and stay out when waves are breaking onto rock.
