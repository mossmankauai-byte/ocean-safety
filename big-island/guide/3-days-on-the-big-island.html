3 Days on the Big Island: An Honest First-Timer Itinerary

Three days on Hawaiʻi Island buys you one side of it. The island covers 4,028 square miles, about 63 percent of the Hawaiian archipelago's combined landmass and more than all the other islands together. Kailua-Kona to Hilo runs roughly two hours each way over a saddle that tops out at 6,632 feet. This plan puts you on the Kona side, gives one full day to Hawaiʻi Volcanoes National Park, and is honest about what gets left out.

Quick facts

  • Hawaiʻi Island covers 4,028 square miles, about 63 percent of the Hawaiian archipelago's combined landmass.
  • Hawaiʻi Volcanoes National Park is about 95 miles and 2 to 2.5 hours from Kailua-Kona on Highway 11, and 30 miles and about 45 minutes from Hilo.
  • Park entrance is $30 per private vehicle, credit or debit only, and the park is open 24 hours a day, 7 days a week.
  • Kīlauea has been erupting inside Halemaʻumaʻu since December 23, 2024, and was at USGS alert level Orange, Watch, in late July 2026.
  • The eruption area is closed; the Park Service directs viewing to caldera-rim overlooks including Uēkahuna, Kīlauea Overlook, Kūpinaʻi Pali and Keanakākoʻi.
  • The Park Service warns that parking lots can be full by 10 a.m.
  • Hilo averages 120.39 inches of rain across 273 days a year, the fourth-wettest city in the United States.
  • The Daniel K. Inouye Highway, or Saddle Road, is 52.7 miles long and reaches a maximum elevation of 6,632 feet.

How to think about three days

Most first-timers try to circle the island and spend the trip in a rental car. Pick one side and take one long day trip out of it. The Daniel K. Inouye Highway, better known as Saddle Road, is 52.7 miles long, tops out at 6,632 feet between Mauna Kea and Mauna Loa, and is the shortest driving route between Hilo and Kailua-Kona.

Hawaiʻi Volcanoes National Park sits about 95 miles southeast of Kailua-Kona on Highway 11, a 2 to 2.5 hour drive each way, according to the National Park Service. Three days means one anchor day, and for most people that is the volcano. If you would rather spend it on the Kohala beaches or Mauna Kea, that is a fair trade. It is still a trade.

Day 1: land and stay close

Kona International Airport is just north of Kailua-Kona town. Get the car, buy water and food, and go to the water in the late afternoon rather than starting a drive. Kamakahonu Beach at Kailua Pier is a small protected cove next to the Kamakahonu royal residence, and about as easy as an arrival day gets. There is no lifeguard there.

Kahaluʻu Beach Park, south of town, has a full-time lifeguard and shallow, reef-protected snorkeling. It also carries a hazard flag in our conditions data, so ask the tower about the current through the reef before you swim out. Check today's conditions first either way.

Day 2: Hawaiʻi Volcanoes National Park

From Kona this is a long day, mostly because of driving. Leave early. Entrance is $30 per private vehicle, credit or debit only, since the park does not accept cash. The park itself is open 24 hours a day, 7 days a week, including holidays.

Kīlauea has been erupting inside Halemaʻumaʻu crater at the summit since December 23, 2024, and as of late July 2026 the USGS alert level is Orange, Watch, with intermittent lava fountaining rather than a steady flow. The eruption area is closed. The Park Service directs viewing to open overlooks along the caldera rim, including Uēkahuna, Kīlauea Overlook at Kūkamāhuākea, Kūpinaʻi Pali and Keanakākoʻi, and warns that parking lots can be full by 10 a.m.

Episodic means it may be doing nothing the day you arrive, so check the USGS Kīlauea update that morning and plan around the rest of the park. The Kīlauea Iki Trail crosses the floor of the crater where the 1959 eruption ponded a lava lake up to 413 feet deep, and Chain of Craters Road runs down the slopes of Kīlauea to the sea. Both hold up with no lava in sight.

Day 3: Kohala sand or a South Kona morning

North gets you the island's widest white-sand beach. Hāpuna Beach is a state recreation area on the Kohala coast with lifeguard service, and it charges non-residents $5 per person plus $10 per vehicle to park. Kaunaʻoa next door is usually calmer but has no lifeguard.

South gets you the better snorkeling. Hōnaunau Bay, called Two Step, sits beside Puʻuhonua o Hōnaunau National Historical Park, which is $20 per private vehicle and also does not accept cash. There is no sand and no lifeguard at Two Step. You step off a lava ledge straight into deep water, which is fine on a flat day and not fine on a swelly one.

Kona or Hilo as a base

Hilo averages 120.39 inches of rain a year across 273 days with measurable precipitation, which makes it the fourth-wettest city in the United States. The Kona side is far drier. That is not a reason to avoid Hilo. It is greener and 30 miles from the volcano, but it is why most short-trip visitors stay on the Kona side.

If the volcano is the entire point of your trip, flip the plan. Fly into Hilo, sleep on that side, and drive 30 miles and about 45 minutes to the park instead of 95 miles and 2 to 2.5 hours.

What does not fit in three days

The Mauna Kea summit road above the 9,200-foot Visitor Information Station is unpaved, rough and quite steep, and the mountain's own guidance is to use 4WD low range to reduce brake failure and overheating on the descent. Check your rental agreement before you build a day around it. Access down Waipiʻo Valley Road is limited to Big Island residents, county-permitted tour operators, and people practicing Native Hawaiian traditional and customary rights.

Papakōlea green sand beach is about 3 miles east of Ka Lae, or South Point, at the far south end of the island, and is legally reachable only on foot over pastureland and eroded, rugged paths. Pololū Valley is at the far north end. Both are worth a morning. Neither is worth what it would cost your three-day driving budget.

Before you go

  • State Parks posts this warning for Hāpuna: dangerous rip currents and pounding shorebreak during periods of high surf, and waves over 3 feet are for experts, with all others staying out. Hawaiʻi's state ocean safety program notes the average wave height when a spinal cord injury occurs is 3 feet. Check live conditions, swim near the tower, and do not turn your back on the water.
  • Most Kona and Kaʻū beaches have no lifeguard at all. When you have the choice, pick a lifeguarded beach and ask the tower about the day's current before you get in.
  • The Park Service warns that hazardous volcanic gasses can endanger sensitive groups, naming those with heart or respiratory issues, infants, children, and pregnant women. Check air quality before and during your visit, and bring eye protection and a dust mask for airborne tephra.
  • The Park Service tells visitors to stay on marked trails and overlooks, never enter closed or restricted areas, and keep a safe distance from earth cracks, cliff edges, vents and fumaroles.
  • The volcano is a long drive from Kona, and the crossing tops out at 6,632 feet where weather changes fast. Fill the tank before you start, and do not drive it worn out after dark.
